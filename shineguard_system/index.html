ShineGuard: a shin guard that watches for overuse injury

A matched pair of smart shin guards that measure impact and motion during a match, and flag when one leg is carrying too much load.

Fits, flexes, and survives a season the way an ordinary shin guard does.

How it works

Each guard senses, judges, and scores before it ever talks to the phone

Each guard runs its own sense-to-decide loop, not just a radio bolted to a chip. A 6-axis IMU samples acceleration and rotation at 200Hz; two thin-film force pads read the shin's contact pressure directly. Both feed an on-chip classifier that separates a real impact from footfall, turf texture, or a knock against the other shin, then rolls the result into a running load index per leg. None of that needs the phone nearby. It only sees a light summary during play, and the full detail after the match.

The IMU is 6-axis, not 9: the magnetometer some IMUs add drifts near steel cleats and turf infill, and heading doesn't help detect an impact anyway. Streaming raw 200Hz over Bluetooth would also blow the power budget below, so the classifier runs on the guard and the radio only carries results.

Validation

The load number has to be checked against a real force, not a feeling

The injury-risk score is only as good as the force number underneath it, so that number gets checked against a reference before anything downstream is trusted.

The rig mounts a molded tibia fixture over a reference load cell, sampled at 5kHz. A 2kg padded mass drops onto a guard clamped to the fixture from five heights, 5 to 40cm, ten drops each, for 50 trials spanning 0.1 to 0.8 J of impact energy, roughly a moderate to hard studs-in plant. The guard's own peak-force estimate is logged alongside the load cell's reading for every drop.

Target: the guard's peak estimate lands within 8% of the reference load cell across all 50 drops. Below 10cm the FSR response flattens near its floor and tends to undercount, so those trials are checked separately, and a miss there flags the low end of the injury-risk scale as unreliable rather than silently reporting a number.

A cheaper second check covers motion: top speed and distance, both from the same IMU, against a photocell timing gate over 20 flying-start sprints, target within 5% of the gate. The two figures worth showing a coach or a judge are below, because a device that lights up proves nothing on its own.

Changes to the plan

Three corrections worth making before any hardware is cut

Stop streaming raw 200Hz over Bluetooth

Streaming raw 200Hz IMU data continuously (2.4 kB/s per guard) would keep the radio active far more of each interval than the 2Hz link the power budget assumes, plausibly doubling draw and cutting the 18-hour target to under 9. Fix: detect peaks on the MCU, notify the phone at 2Hz, and reserve full-rate export for a burst sync of only the ±200ms window around each impact.

Cut the EMG channel, keep two force pads

The app lists an "EMG Pressure" sensor, but dry-contact surface EMG needs stable skin-electrode impedance, and a sock sliding against sweaty skin for 70 minutes pushes that impedance up by an order of magnitude, burying the muscle signal in motion artifact. What the injury-risk score actually needs is normal force at the shin, which a pair of thin-film force sensors reads directly with no skin contact. Cut EMG, keep two FSR pads.

Move heart rate off the shin guard

Heart rate can't be read reliably from a shin: photoplethysmography needs steady skin contact, shin skin is thin over bone with little perfusion signal, and sprinting doubles the motion artifact a wrist or chest sensor already fights. Recommendation: pair a standard BLE chest strap and merge its stream in the same app session, rather than taking on a sensor channel the shin guard can't deliver.

What could go wrong

The wiring is a weekend. Telling a real hit from noise is the work.

The genuinely hard part is teaching the classifier to tell a real studs-in plant from turf texture, a shin-to-shin knock, or a hard sprint start, since all three throw a similar spike into a 6-axis IMU. That needs a short per-player calibration capture, not one global threshold.

If the FSR pads prove noisy under sweat and strap-pressure variance by the six-week bench-test checkpoint, fall back to an accelerometer-only load proxy, already supported in published stress-fracture research, and report relative loading only until the pads are reworked.

Slide tackles flex the shell enough to fatigue flex-PCB solder joints and loosen an FSR's adhesive backing over a season, and sweat past the strap seam corrodes the pogo contacts if the shell isn't potted well.

The details

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project.

Signal path and state machine
  • Sleep: accelerometer wake-on-motion, threshold 0.3g, 180μA total draw
  • Active session: IMU at 200Hz, FSR pair polled at 100Hz, on-chip peak/impact detection
  • BLE notify at 2Hz during play: speed, per-limb load index, merged HR from the paired chest strap
  • Full 200Hz waveform lives only in the flash ring buffer; long-term storage keeps just the ±200ms window around each detected event, plus a 1Hz summary stream
  • End-of-session burst sync over BLE, roughly 2 minutes at a larger MTU
Data budget
  • Raw 200Hz × 6 axes × 2 bytes = 2.4 kB/s per guard; a 70-minute match at that rate is 10MB, too big to keep, which is why it isn't
  • 1Hz summary log: ~40 B/sample × 4,200s ≈ 168 kB/match
  • Impact event buffers: ~80 events/match × 400ms window × 200Hz × 6 axes × 2 bytes ≈ 77 kB/match
  • Total ≈ 245 kB/match against a 4MB flash budget, about 16 matches of headroom before a sync is required

Bring-up order
  • Battery + charge IC + LDO alone: verify regulation and the charge curve from the dock
  • MCU alone: clock, flash blink
  • IMU alone over a bench UART harness: verify the raw 200Hz stream
  • FSR + ADC divider alone: calibrate the force-to-voltage curve against known weights
  • BLE advertising alone: bench range and RSSI check
  • Flash write/read alone: buffer timing
  • Full integration on the bench: sensors through edge detection to flash to BLE
  • Field bring-up: worn static, then walking, then the drop-rig validation above
Validation checklist
  • Drop-rig vs. reference load cell: 50 trials, within 8%
  • Sprint speed vs. photocell gate: 20 sprints, within 5%
  • Battery rundown at 15mA continuous load: confirm ≥17h to empty
  • Saline soak, 30 minutes: no continuity fault after, a simulated sweat-exposure check
  • Strap-flex fatigue rig: 20 full-match cycles, inspect flex-PCB continuity and FSR adhesion after
What this device cannot do
  • Not a medical device and not FDA-cleared; it flags elevated mechanical loading, it does not diagnose a stress fracture or a shin splint
  • Does not measure true muscle activation; the EMG channel was cut, see the changes above
  • Carries no heart-rate sensor itself; HR requires a separately worn, separately charged BLE chest strap
  • Accuracy figures come from controlled bench drops, not a clinical study tying sensor readings to real injury outcomes
  • Untested on turf types and cleat-stud patterns outside the bench test surface
