Ski hardware

SlopeSense tracks how you actually skied

A chest-mounted sensor that logs speed, route and vertical descent all day, then turns the log into stats and a few concrete suggestions once you're back at the lodge.

No phone on the hill, and it has to survive a full day below freezing.

How it works

Five sensors, one chip, no screen

SenseGPS, IMU and altimeter sample
ProcessESP32-S3 timestamps and fuses data
RecordWritten to onboard microSD
SyncPulled to phone after skiing
DecideApp turns log into stats

The one step that is not obvious: GPS speed comes from the Doppler shift on the satellite signal, not from differencing two position fixes, which is why it holds to about 0.1 m/s even when the position itself wanders a few metres under trees. Everything is buffered on the pod; the phone is needed only once, after the lift closes, to pull the day's log over Bluetooth.

How we know it works

Checked against a radar gun, not a spec sheet

The GPS chip's datasheet claims 0.1 m/s velocity accuracy from Doppler shift, which is a lab number, not a ski-hill one. Trees, valley walls and a skier's own body blocking the antenna at speed all degrade a real fix, so the number that matters is what the pod reads when someone is actually skiing.

The check: a handheld radar gun (accurate to ±1 mph on its own spec) stationed at the base of a groomed run, aimed through a 30 m timed gate. A skier wearing the pod, plus a Garmin ski watch as a second reference, passes through the gate at a range of speeds across a full day.

Acceptance number: mean absolute speed error under 3% against the radar gun, sampled over 40 passes across 4 sessions spanning 15 to 55 km/h. Route distance over a full day, checked against the Garmin watch, has to agree within 5%.

If it misses: a consistent bias across all speeds points to antenna placement or multipath off the jacket fabric, fixed with a per-unit calibration offset written to flash. A bias that only shows up in trees or narrow valleys is a coverage limit, not a bug, and gets called out to the user in the app rather than papered over.

Reference accuracy figures: u-blox MAX-M10 series datasheet (GPS Doppler velocity), Bushnell Velocity Speed Gun specification (±1 mph).

Changes to the original plan

Three corrections, all in scope before build

Cut: real-time edge-angle coaching

A natural next feature is telling a skier their lean angle mid-turn, the way pressure-insole products do. Validating a ±1° edge-angle claim needs a synchronized motion-capture reference, which is out of reach for a season's build. We are shipping GPS speed, route and vertical descent only, all of which validate against a radar gun and a consumer GPS watch. Edge angle stays a stretch goal, not a claim.

Correct: speed alerts should not be a fixed number

The original idea used one global "too fast" threshold, borrowed from general ski-safety guidance. The same 40 km/h is cautious on an empty green run and reckless on an icy, crowded blue one. The fix: the app flags a run only when a skier's own speed sits above the 90th percentile of their last 10 sessions on comparable terrain, not against a number nobody calibrated to the slope.

Add: charging and regulation, missing from the first parts list

The original list had sensors and a battery but no path to charge it or step 3.7 V down cleanly to the 3.3 V the MCU and GPS need. Added: an MCP73831 charge IC behind a USB-C port, and an AP2112 regulator rated to 600 mA against a combined draw near 60 mA, leaving headroom for the microSD's 100 mA write bursts without browning out the GPS mid-fix.

What could go wrong

The hard part is telling a real turn from noise

Wiring five sensors to one MCU is a weekend. Writing firmware that reliably tells "start of run" from "still fiddling with boot buckles," or a real speed spike from a GPS glitch bouncing off a lift tower, is the actual work, and it needs a full season of logged runs to tune.

Safety valve: if the ceramic patch antenna cannot hold a fix through tree cover by early December, fall back to a puck antenna mounted on the shoulder strap instead of the chest pod, which trades a slightly bulkier cable for a clearer sky view. That decision has to be made by the first test weekend, not mid-season.

The dull failure mode: an elastic chest strap loosens over a full day of falls and re-buckling, and a pod that has slid down to the sternum reads worse GPS and noisier IMU data than one that never moved. A strap with a positive mechanical stop, not just friction, is the fix.

For the specialist

Build detail

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project.

Detection and processing

Descent detection: state machine on vertical speed (from the altimeter) and GPS speed. States: idle → lift/traverse (speed < 5 km/h or steady altitude) → run (sustained descent, GPS speed > 8 km/h for 5 s) → stopped (speed < 2 km/h for 8 s). Run boundaries are what the app uses to segment stats per descent.

IMU sampled at 200 Hz but only summary statistics (peak vertical acceleration, RMS vibration) are logged per 1 s window, not the raw stream, to keep the log small. GPS logged at its native 5 Hz throughout, including lift rides, since that is what confirms route.

Data format and sync

One CSV per day on the microSD: timestamp, latitude, longitude, GPS speed, GPS altitude, barometric altitude, IMU summary fields, run-state flag. Roughly 5 Hz effective row rate, about 3 MB for a 6 hour day, well inside a 32 GB card for a full season.

BLE sync is a bulk file transfer of the day's CSV once the phone app is opened and paired, not a live stream. No live telemetry to the app is implemented in this version.

Power budget
  • GPS tracking: 25 mA typical (u-blox MAX-M10S continuous tracking mode)
  • IMU: 1 mA at 200 Hz
  • Altimeter: 1 mA at 25 Hz
  • MCU: 80 mA during BLE sync, under 1 mA in deep sleep between logging ticks
  • microSD: 100 mA peak during a write burst, roughly 2% duty cycle
  • Blended average: about 60 mA
  • 1200 mAh cell ÷ 60 mA ≈ 20 h at 85% regulator efficiency ≈ 17 h
  • Cold derate (LiPo capacity drops roughly 20% near 0°C): ≈ 14 h effective, against an 8 h ski day
Bring-up order

Power rail first, confirm 3.3 V under load with a bench supply before any sensor is attached. Then GPS alone, confirm a fix outdoors. Then IMU alone over I2C, confirm register reads. Then baro alone. Then all three together on the shared bus, checking for address conflicts. Then microSD, confirm write speed under the logging rate before sealing the enclosure. Then BLE pairing, last, since it is the least safety-critical link.

Validation checklist
  • 40 radar-gun passes across 4 sessions, 15-55 km/h, mean absolute error under 3%
  • Full-day route distance within 5% of a Garmin ski watch worn simultaneously
  • 8 hour continuous logging test at -10°C in a chest freezer before the first ski day
  • Drop test of the enclosure from 1 m onto packed snow, 10 drops
  • IP66 spray test after gasket assembly, before first use
What this device cannot do

No live coaching or alerts while skiing; all feedback is after the day ends. No edge-angle, carving-technique or turn-shape analysis. No lane-level route accuracy: two parallel trails within about 5 m of each other may read as the same line. No indoor or under-canopy fix; a lost signal shows as a gap in the log, not a guess. Altitude drifts with weather and needs the user to confirm the base elevation once each day.
