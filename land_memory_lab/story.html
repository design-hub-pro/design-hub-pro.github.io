A LAND MEMORY LAB STORY

The creek that refused to disappear

For a hundred years it flowed in the dark beneath the city. This is how a 172-year-old survey map brought it back to the light.

01 — Before the city

A spring-fed creek runs to the sea

In 1852, U.S. Coast Survey teams walked this basin and drew every rivulet by hand. Lobos Creek gathered rain filtered through the dunes and carried it, clean and cold, straight to the Pacific. The Ohlone had drawn water here for millennia.

02 — The taking

The army taps the spring

By 1912 the creek was plumbed to supply the Presidio garrison. Reservoirs and pipes carved up the flow. Downstream, the channel was straightened and walled — the first step in a slow disappearance repeated across the city's 48 creeks.

03 — The dark century

Buried, but never gone

As the neighborhood hardened, most sister creeks vanished into culverts. Lobos held on in a concrete ditch — degraded, invasive-choked, forgotten. Yet the water never stopped. Every storm, the old channel reminded the land where it wanted to go.

04 — Daylight

Following the 1852 map back

In 2002, restorers overlaid the original survey on modern LiDAR and rebuilt 400 metres of natural channel. Native willow and rush returned; the creek runs to a wild beach once more — the only free-flowing stream left in San Francisco.
