Smart medicine dispenser · system design

DoseCheck

A pill dispenser that releases each dose at its set time, weighs it, and tells the family if it is still sitting there.

It has to keep working through a power cut and a Wi-Fi outage. About $82 in parts.

How it works

One locked carousel, one scale under the cup

ClockA scheduled dose time arrives
ReleaseCarousel turns one slot
WeighCup weight checked against expected
PromptLight and buzzer call the patient
WatchCup lifted and returned empty?
AlertUntouched at 30 minutes: caregiver told
RecordLog drives the refill countdown

Only the slot over the drop hole can empty, so a dose cannot come out early. Refill prediction is subtraction: pills left divided by pills per day, corrected by the log of doses actually taken.

Assumed: up to four doses a day, 28 slots, loaded weekly by a family member. The phone app is a separate design.

How we know it works

Every feature rests on one number: the weight of the cup

It gets checked against a 0.001 g pocket scale and calibration masses.

Weight. Five masses from 100 mg to 10 g, 20 placements each. The target is within ±10 mg of the reference on every one. The device tares seconds before each dose drops, so slow drift cancels and only short-term noise counts.

Dose errors. 200 staged dispenses with real over-the-counter tablets, 80 mg to 1 g each, in doses up to 2 g. Half are correct. Half carry one seeded error: a pill removed, added, or swapped. The target is 99 of 100 errors caught where the pill involved weighs 100 mg or more, with at most 1 false alarm in 100 correct doses. Two different pills of the same weight will pass. The app says so plainly.

Missed doses. 100 trials filmed at 30 fps: cup taken, cup left, cup bumped, some pills left behind. The video is the truth. Every untouched dose must raise the alarm at 30 minutes, and no bump may count as taken.

Mechanics and alerts. 20 full turns, 560 dispenses, counted by hand: zero double drops, at most one jam. Caregiver alerts must reach the phone within 60 seconds, timed on 50 alerts.

If weight misses ±10 mg, the first fix is a draught shield and a longer averaging window. If it still misses, the smallest detectable pill rises to 150 mg and this page gets corrected. The seeded-error table is the strongest thing to show a judge.

Changes to the original plan

Three changes, one of them a cut

Cut the pill camera. Check the medicine by weight.

A wrong-medicine check suggests a camera that recognises pills. Most tablets are white and round, told apart only by an imprint pressed into one face. Each pill lands imprint-up about half the time, so a four-pill dose is fully readable in 0.5⁴ = 6% of drops. A check that answers 6% of the time is worse than none. Weight catches a missing, extra, or swapped pill, and the locked carousel handles wrong time. The cut saves about $10 and three weeks of model training.

One scale, not 28 sensors

"Detect if the medicine is still there" reads as a sensor in every compartment: 28 sensors and 28 wires routed through a turning carousel. One 100 g load cell under the cup does the job. Its noise after tare is about 10 mg against a lightest pill of 80 mg, and lifting the 3 g cup is a signal 300 times the noise. It also reports how much was left behind, which a beam sensor cannot.

Add a battery and a clock

The brief has no backup power and takes its time from Wi-Fi. A power cut or a router fault would skip doses silently, which is the failure this device exists to prevent. A DS3231 clock holds time to about a minute a year. One 18650 cell gives 9.4 Wh × 0.85 ÷ 0.3 W idle = about 26 hours. The 5 V rail peaks at 680 mA (motor 240, Wi-Fi burst 350, LEDs 60, buzzer 30) against 2 A available. Added cost: $18.

What could go wrong

The weight logic, the fallback, and pill dust

The hard part is the weight logic, not the wiring. A hand resting on the platform, a cup put back with one pill in it, a capsule held in the chute by static: each looks like something else to a simple threshold.

The safety valve is an infrared break-beam pair across the cup, $3, bought now and kept in the drawer. It reports present or absent only, so missed-dose detection survives and the weight check is lost. If the load cell has not passed ±10 mg by 31 October 2026, switch.

Pill dust. Uncoated tablets shed powder that builds up on the deck and chute until the carousel drags or a pill hangs. Humidity makes gelatin capsules tacky. Keep it out of the bathroom, and have the app ask for a wipe-down at each weekly load.

For the engineer

Build detail

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project.

Dose state machine and weight check

IDLE → TARE → TURN → SETTLE → CHECK → WAIT → TAKEN, MISSED or FAULT.

TARE: mean of 20 samples (2 s), cup must be present and within 0.3 g of its stored weight. TURN: 141 half-steps at 500 steps/s, about 0.3 s, no weighing while the motor runs. SETTLE: 2 s. CHECK: mean of 20 samples.

Expected weight = sum of learned pill masses for that slot. Each pill type is learned once at setup by weighing 10 in the cup. Tolerance = the larger of 30 mg or 3.5% of the dose. Outside tolerance: red light, "do not take" push to both phones, caregiver decides.

WAIT: reading below −1.5 g means cup lifted. Cup back within 0.1 g of tare = TAKEN. Cup back heavier = partial, logged with grams left. A spike shorter than 1 s that returns to the dose weight is a bump and is ignored. Prompts at 0, 10 and 20 minutes. At 30 minutes: MISSED, caregiver push. The window is set per medicine in the app, 30 minutes by default.

Carousel and refill arithmetic

29 pockets: 28 doses and one blank that parks over the drop hole when idle and during loading. 28BYJ-48 gives about 4,076 half-steps per turn, so 140.6 per pocket. Carry the fraction in firmware and re-zero on the hall switch once per turn.

Device supply: filled slots ÷ doses per day. Bottle supply: pills entered at pharmacy fill, minus pills loaded, per medicine. Refill alert when bottle supply falls under 7 days, adjustable. Missed doses stay in the count, so the date moves with real use.

Power budget

Idle at 5 V: ESP32 with Wi-Fi modem sleep about 40 mA, DevKit regulator and USB chip 10 mA, UPS board 5 mA, sensors under 5 mA. Total about 60 mA, 0.3 W. HX711 powered down between doses.

Battery: 2600 mAh × 3.6 V = 9.4 Wh, × 0.85 boost efficiency = 8 Wh, ÷ 0.3 W = about 26 hours. Four dispenses a day add under 0.01 Wh. Peak 680 mA against 2 A: margin of almost three times.

Data and the link to the app

Event record in flash: timestamp, slot, expected mg, measured mg, state, lid flag. About 40 bytes, 4 a day. A 1 MB partition holds years.

Setup over Bluetooth LE. After that, MQTT over TLS to a small broker, which forwards pushes to patient and caregiver. The schedule lives on the device. With Wi-Fi down it still dispenses, prompts and logs, then uploads the backlog.

Bring-up order

Each part alone before any two together. 1: load cell and HX711 on the bench, log an hour of noise and drift. 2: stepper and hall switch, 100 turns, count lost steps. 3: DS3231, check against network time after a week. 4: UPS board, pull the plug under motor load and watch for a reset. 5: chute, by hand, with every pill shape to be used. 6: state machine over serial. 7: Wi-Fi and alerts last.

Validation checklist

Weight within ±10 mg, 5 masses × 20. Seeded errors: 99 of 100 caught, at most 1 false alarm in 100. Missed dose: 100 filmed trials, no misses, no bump counted as taken. 560 dispenses: zero double drops, at most one jam. Alert latency under 60 s on 50 alerts. Power pulled mid-dispense ten times: no reset, no lost slot. 24 hours on battery. Clock drift under 2 s a week.

Smaller changes to the brief

Refill prediction needs no learning model: 28 slots at 2 a day is 14 days, known on day one. The missed-dose window is per medicine, not one fixed number. The lid is logged, not locked.

What this device cannot do

It cannot tell that a pill was swallowed, only that the cup came back empty. It cannot tell apart two different pills of the same weight. In doses over 2 g the smallest detectable error rises above 100 mg, and the app shows the figure for each dose. It does not handle liquids, halved tablets, or medicines kept in the fridge. It is not tamper-proof and is not suitable for controlled drugs. It is a student prototype, not a certified medical device, and it must not be the only safeguard for anyone whose missed dose is an emergency.
