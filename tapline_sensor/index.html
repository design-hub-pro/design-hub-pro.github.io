Hardware · Tapline S1

A water sensor that fits under the sink

It reads how hard the tap water is on every draw and sends the number to the phone.

Battery powered, about $88 in parts, and it must not leak.

How it works

It measures how well the water carries current

SenseTwo graphite rods in the flow
Drive1 kHz square wave, both directions
CorrectWater temperature from an NTC
WaitFirst 20 seconds thrown away
DecideMedian of 512 samples
SendBluetooth to the phone

A reed impeller wakes the board when someone opens the tap. Nothing draws power between draws.

The electrodes are driven in alternating directions because direct current plates ions onto the graphite and the reading walks away within seconds. The first 20 seconds of a draw are discarded because water that has been standing in the pipe reads high.

How we know it works

Every recommendation rests on one number, so that number gets checked hard

The device is checked against three references. A calibrated conductivity meter, itself set with 84 and 1413 µS/cm standards. An EDTA titration kit, which gives the true hardness of a sample. A 1 litre jug on a kitchen scale for the flow total.

The rig is a 20 litre reservoir spiked with calcium chloride and magnesium sulphate to cover 40 to 260 ppm, pumped through the unit and the reference probe in series. Twelve concentrations, five water temperatures from 8 to 35 °C, twenty repeat draws at each point.

Four numbers decide whether it ships. Conductivity within 3% of the reference between 100 and 1000 µS/cm. Hardness within a mean absolute error of 12 ppm against titration, with R² of 0.95 or better. Flow total within 3% across twenty draws of 0.5 to 5 litres. Drift under 2% over a 14 day soak with the unit plumbed in and running.

If the hardness target is missed, the app stops printing a ppm figure and shows a three band label instead: soft, moderate, hard. Every recommendation except the dishwasher salt level still works from the band, so a miss costs one feature rather than the product.

The agreement plot from that rig, twelve points against titration, is the strongest thing to put in front of a judge or a buyer.

Changes to the plan

Three things in the brief do not survive contact with the water

It cannot clamp on

The app calls it a clamp-on sensor. Conductivity needs two electrodes wetted by the water, so a clamped device can only read flow and pipe temperature. It becomes a G3/8 in-line body fitted between the angle valve and the tap hose. That is a ten minute job with two wrenches and a bowl, and it adds a brass tee, two O-rings and a shut-off step to the setup flow.

Cut the chlorine and pH cells

An amperometric free chlorine cell runs $150 to $220, wants fresh electrolyte monthly, and holds ±0.05 mg/L only at a steady flow. A kitchen tap runs 4 to 9 L/min in bursts. A glass pH electrode cannot be stored dry and needs two point calibration every month. Both move to a monthly test strip photographed by the phone, which resolves the 0.2, 0.5 and 1.0 mg/L bands. That is all the shower filter decision needs.

Hardness is inferred, so ship the band

Conductivity counts every ion, not calcium and magnesium alone. Inside one supply zone the ratio is stable, so a single titration at install fits the slope, usually 0.45 to 0.55 ppm per µS/cm on a carbonate supply. Show the estimate with a ±12 ppm band and re-fit when the utility switches source. A bare number with no band is a claim this hardware cannot support.

Two smaller corrections are in the appendix: the flow sensor has to be a reed type rather than a powered hall sensor, and the 3.3 V rail needs a buck-boost converter rather than a regulator.

What could go wrong

The wiring is a weekend. The judgement is the work.

The hard part is deciding whether a plateau is real. A part open tap, an air slug after the mains has been off, and a genuine reading all look similar for the first few seconds. The rule is a plateau that moves less than 6 µS/cm across the window, and tuning that number against real draws is where the time goes.

The safety valve is a ready-made industrial EC probe, about $28 with a known cell constant, which drops into the same body. If the graphite pair drifts more than 2% across the 14 day soak, switch. That decision is made at the end of week 4, not later.

The failure nobody plans for is a compression joint that weeps behind a closed cabinet door. Fit a $3 leak pad under the unit, and pressure test into a bowl for twenty minutes before the door goes back on.

For the engineer

Build detail

Working notes. Not needed to understand the project.

Measurement method and parameters

Excitation: 1 kHz square wave, 3.3 Vpp, alternating polarity every half cycle, series 1 kΩ sense resistor, 100 nF DC block in line with each rod. Gap 3 mm, wetted length 18 mm, cell constant fitted at install rather than assumed.

Sampling: 12 bit ADC, 64 samples per burst, 25 bursts per second. Median of the last 512 samples inside the window. Temperature compensation 2.0% per °C, referred to 25 °C.

Reject rules: plateau movement over 6 µS/cm across the window, flow under 1.5 L/min, water temperature outside 4 to 40 °C, or fewer than 380 valid samples.

State machine and data format

States: SLEEP, WAKE (reed interrupt), FLUSH (20 s), SAMPLE (20 s), SETTLE, STORE, ADVERTISE, SLEEP. A draw shorter than 25 s stores litres only and never produces a reading.

Record: 24 bytes. Timestamp, EC in µS/cm, temperature in 0.1 °C, litres in 0.01 L, sample count, reject code, battery millivolts. 30 days at 12 reads a day is 8.6 kB, which sits in flash with room to spare.

Transport: BLE 5.0 notify, 3 second advertise window after each read. The phone owns the EC to hardness slope, so the firmware never needs a calibration update.

Power budget

Cell 3000 mAh at 3.6 V, 10.8 Wh. Buck-boost at 88% gives about 2880 mAh usable at 3.3 V.

Idle 52 µA, so 1.25 mAh a day. Twelve reads at 45 s and 45 mA average, 6.8 mAh a day. Eight short draws totalled at 12 mA, 1.6 mAh a day. Total 9.6 mAh a day, about 290 days. The recommendation to the owner is six months, which leaves 40% margin.

Two corrections from the original list. A powered hall flow sensor draws 12 to 15 mA continuously, which is 340 mAh a day and empties the cell in nine days, so the flow sensor must be a reed type. A 3.3 V regulator drops out near 3.45 V and abandons the bottom third of the cell, so the rail needs a buck-boost.

Calibration at install

Two point EC calibration with 84 and 1413 µS/cm standards in a cup, rods dipped, five minutes.

One EDTA titration on a glass drawn from the same tap. Enter the result in the app. The app fits the slope through that point and the measured EC, and stores it per household. Re-run when the water utility posts a source change, or when a read sits more than 25 ppm off the seven day median.

Bring-up order

Test every part alone before wiring any two together. Board blinks and advertises. Charger holds 4.2 V with the cell disconnected. Buck-boost holds 3.3 V while a bench supply is swept 3.0 to 4.2 V. Reed sensor counts pulses against a jug. NTC reads room temperature within 0.5 °C. Electrode pair in a standard solution before it ever sees a pipe. Body pressure tested dry, then wet in a bowl, then under the sink.

Validation checklist

Twelve concentrations from 40 to 260 ppm, twenty draws each. Five temperatures from 8 to 35 °C at 120 ppm. Twenty jug draws from 0.5 to 5 L. Fourteen day soak with a read every two hours, drift measured against a fresh standard at the end. Ten deliberate bad draws, part open tap and air slug, all of which must be rejected. One full discharge to confirm the endurance number.

What this device cannot do

It cannot measure calcium and magnesium separately, so it cannot report hardness without the per-home fit.

It cannot see lead, copper, nitrate, PFAS or bacteria. None of those change conductivity enough to be seen, and no one should read a green grade on this device as a safety statement.

It cannot measure free chlorine or pH continuously, by decision. Those come from a monthly strip.

It reads one line only. Hot water, the shower feed and an outside tap each need their own unit.

It is not a leak detector. It sees flow only when the tap on its own branch is open.
