What the game reads while you cook

Every level produces timing and choice data on its own. Nobody is asked to recall a word list. The steps a player takes, the order they take them in, and how they recover after an interruption are the measurement.

Planning

Does the roast go in before the salad is chopped? The planning board captures the order, and the number of times it gets rearranged.

Working memory

Three ingredients are shown, then hidden. The pantry search records what was found, what was missed, and how often the list was reopened.

Task switching

The phone rings mid-recipe. The cost of that switch is the gap between the last step before the call and the first step after it.

Attention

Pantry shelves hold roughly 40 items. Search path and time to first correct item separate a slow search from a distracted one.

Error correction

A pan starts to smoke. What matters is not whether it was noticed but how long it took, and whether the fix was the right one.

Processing speed

Tap-to-action latency across hundreds of routine interactions per session, held separate from motor slowness where the input allows.

Two things the brief overstates

A single Cognitive Performance Score is the weakest part of the design.

Nine separate abilities collapsed into one number hides the thing a family actually needs to see, which is which one moved. Show the six sub-scores as the primary view. If a headline number is wanted for motivation, label it as a game score, not a clinical one.

Adaptive difficulty and longitudinal measurement pull against each other.

If the game gets easier when a player struggles, a flat score line can mean either steady ability or a quietly falling difficulty. The fix is a fixed calibration level, same recipe and same interruptions, run every two weeks outside the adaptive loop. Everything else can adapt freely.

On scope.

The deliverable list runs to ten items including a research paper and a public pilot. A realistic first year is the game, the six sub-scores, family recipe mode, and a caregiver view. Nothing in this design claims diagnostic use, and it should not be presented that way to families.

Design detail (not needed to understand the project)

Genre and view. Isometric single-screen simulation. No scrolling camera, no dexterity requirement, no fail state that ends a run. Target session is 8 to 15 minutes.

Accessibility floor. Minimum 18pt UI type, 4.5:1 contrast on every label, tap targets at 48px, no reliance on color alone for state, and full audio narration of every instruction. The primary device is a tablet held flat or propped on a table.

Family recipe mode. A photographed recipe card is parsed into steps and timings, then checked by the user before it becomes playable. Parsing errors are expected and the confirm step is what makes the mode safe.

Data. Event-level logs of every tap, step transition, timer state, and interruption, timestamped to the session. Research export is anonymized at the account level and opt-in, separate from the caregiver share, which is per-person and revocable.

Art direction. Warm hand-painted isometric assets in terracotta, butter yellow, and sage. The visual target is a familiar kitchen rather than a clinical tool, because the whole premise depends on the player not feeling tested.
