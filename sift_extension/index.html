Sift

Browser extension · Chrome and Edge · prototype

Open the landing page See it filtering a thread

A volume knob for your feed, not a filter on the internet.

Sift is a browser extension that scores comments on the page before you read them and turns down the ones built to hurt. Nothing is deleted, nothing is reported, and one click shows you anything it hid. It runs on your own machine, so the text you read never reaches a server.

Eight screens below, from the install page through to the panel that shows where the model is getting things wrong. That last one is the point of the project.

You hold the dial

Per category thresholds, per site rules, and a reveal button on every hidden comment. No platform policy has to change.

Scoring happens in the tab

TensorFlow.js by default, about 41 ms a comment. Google's Perspective API is available and clearly marked as a privacy trade.

The bias is on screen, not buried

A fairness panel shows your own disagreement rate and a local probe set, with citations to the research on dialect bias.

Four things we changed from the brief

The plan as written would build, but four parts of it would cause trouble in week three. Here is what we did instead and why.

One threshold becomes four

The brief hides anything scoring above 0.8. That number is wrong for both ends of the range. A threat missed at 0.7 is a real cost to the user. An insult hidden at 0.8 is where nearly all the false positives live. Sift ships one threshold per category, with threats at 0.60 and insults at 0.80.

"Retrain the model" is oversold

A few hundred taps on "this isn't toxic" cannot retrain a neural classifier, in a browser or anywhere else. What fits is a thin logistic layer over the base model's embeddings that shifts the decision boundary for text resembling what you corrected. The fairness panel says exactly that, in those words. Promising more would be the kind of claim a judge takes apart in thirty seconds.

Friendship has to be scoped down

An extension cannot see whether two strangers are friends. It has no access to their messages or their follow graph. What it can see is your own behaviour on pages you visited, so Sift counts how many times you have replied to an account and lifts that account's threshold by at most 0.15. Threats ignore the lift entirely, because the person hurting you is often someone you talk to.

Cool Down breaks the privacy promise

Rewriting a heated reply needs a model far larger than anything that runs in a tab, which means your draft goes to a server. That directly contradicts the claim Phase 1 is built on. So Cool Down is off by default, asks before its first run, and is off entirely on sites where you turned it off. Do not bolt it onto the same switch as the filter.

One thing to check before week two. Perspective API's default free quota is low, and a busy comment page can need dozens of scores in a few seconds. Confirm the current quota and the process for raising it before designing the demo around the hosted engine. The local engine has no such limit, which is a second reason it is the default here.

Four weeks, and what "done" means each week

This follows the brief's roadmap with one change: the fairness work moves into week three rather than being left for later. It is the part that makes the project interesting, and it is also the part that gets cut when a schedule slips.

Week 1

Read the page

Manifest V3 skeleton, one content script, one site adapter for Reddit. A MutationObserver that keeps up with infinite scroll.

Done when: the console prints every comment on a thread, including ones loaded after scrolling, with no duplicates.

Week 2

Score and hide

TensorFlow.js toxicity model wired in, batched per animation frame. Blur, category label, score, and a reveal button on every hidden node.

Done when: a 200 comment thread scores in under three seconds without dropping frames, and nothing is hidden without a visible way back.

Week 3

Be wrong in public

The correction button, the local logistic layer, the probe set, and the fairness panel. Write the 600 probe sentences by hand and get someone outside the project to check them.

Done when: you can state a measured false positive gap between two text styles, and show that corrections move it.

Week 4

Cool Down

Reply rewriting through a hosted model, behind its own consent screen and its own switch. Three tones, a visible diff, and no posting permission.

Done when: a rewrite keeps the original argument in a blind read by three people who were not told which version was which.

What to cut if time runs short

Week 4. Cool Down is the most impressive demo and the least defensible piece of the project: it needs a hosted model, it breaks the privacy claim, and its output is hard to evaluate honestly. A finished week 3 with a measured bias number beats a half-built rewriter. If week 4 survives, ship it as a separate, clearly labelled feature.

Assumptions in this design

All numbers on the dashboard and fairness screens are sample data from a one week pilot pattern, not measurements. The 41 ms figure assumes a recent laptop with WebGL available. Site adapters assume current markup on four sites, which will break. Nothing here has been tested with a user who is actually being harassed, and that is the first thing worth fixing.
