Instrument · figure skating

Quarter

A sensor that straps to one skating boot and measures how far the skater actually turned in the air, how long they were off the ice, and how hard they came down.

It is named after the quarter turn, because that is the number that decides a jump. A jump landed more than a quarter turn short of its full rotation is marked down, and a skater cannot feel the difference between 340 degrees and 360. The device exists to put a number on that gap.

Constraints: it has to survive a landing, work in a rink at around minus 3 degrees with no phone and no wifi, and cost under a hundred dollars a unit.

How it works

Six steps between the boot and the number

Everything happens on the pod. There is no phone in the loop during a session, because a skater is not going to carry one and a rink is a bad place to rely on a radio link.

SenseAn inertial sensor reads three rates of turn and three accelerations, continuously.
DetectA drop in vertical load marks takeoff. A shock marks landing. The gap between them is a flight.
IntegrateTurn rate is added up across the flight to give total degrees turned.
ClassifyThe event is sorted into jump, spin, stroke or fall. Not into jump type.
ScoreAir time becomes height. Degrees become revolutions and a shortfall figure.
LogRaw and summary both go to a card, so a disputed number can be re-checked later.

A skater does a run-through. Afterwards the card holds a row per jump: air time, height, revolutions, degrees short or long, and peak landing load. That is the whole product.

How we know it works

The accuracy claim, and how it gets checked

This is the part of the project that is worth showing to a judge or a reviewer, and it is the part that almost nobody builds. It is easy to make a box that lights up when a skater jumps. It is much harder to say what the box is accurate to, and to have the evidence.

Quarter is checked against two references. On the bench it is checked against a turntable that turns at a known, counted rate. On the ice it is checked against slow motion video at 240 frames a second, which resolves 4.2 milliseconds a frame and lets a jump be counted by hand, frame by frame.

The five numbers we have to hit

The scale check is the one that makes the rest meaningful. One percent on the bench is 11 degrees on a triple.

Why 20 degrees is the number that matters

A jump is marked down when it lands more than 90 degrees short. If the device is good to 20 degrees, a jump that is 40 degrees short can be called short with confidence, and a jump that is 88 degrees short cannot. That is not a weakness to hide. It is the honest edge of the instrument, and the device is built to say so.

The device abstains near the line. Inside a 30 degree band either side of the quarter turn mark, Quarter reports "borderline" and gives the raw degree figure rather than a verdict. An instrument that says "I do not know" in the region where it does not know is more useful than one that guesses, and a reviewer will recognise that immediately.

Counting the jumps by hand, once

The video work is the expensive part and it is not optional. Sixty jumps, counted frame by frame, is roughly six hours of somebody's evening. That labour is the entire basis of every accuracy claim on this page, so it is scheduled as work, not assumed.

Skaters under 18 appear in that video. The footage is taken with the coach and a guardian agreeing in writing, is used only to build the check set, is never published, and is deleted once the numbers are extracted.

If it misses the targets

If rotation accuracy lands at 40 degrees rather than 20, the quarter turn call is dropped from the product and Quarter ships as an air time, height and landing load logger. That is still a useful instrument, because jump height trend and landing load across a season are things a coach currently has no way to measure at all. The project does not fail if the hardest claim fails.

Changes to the original plan

Five things in the brief that do not survive the arithmetic

A brief that says "a smart sensor to track figure skating performance" hides four or five decisions inside the word "performance". Here is what we would change before ordering anything.

1. Cut spin centering. The physics does not allow it.

Coaches would love a number for how far a spin travels across the ice. Getting position from an inertial sensor means integrating acceleration twice, and the error grows with the square of time. A realistic accelerometer bias after calibration is about 5 milli-g, which is 0.049 metres per second squared. Over a six second spin that is one half times 0.049 times 36, or 0.88 metres of drift. Coaches care about travel of 10 to 30 centimetres. The error is three to nine times the thing being measured.

It gets worse. A one degree error in knowing which way is down leaks 0.17 metres per second squared of gravity into the horizontal axes, giving 3.1 metres over the same spin. Spin travel needs a camera or a floor system. Cut it, and say why on the poster, because knowing why a measurement is impossible is itself a result.

2. Do not count rotations off the boot's vertical axis. It manufactures faults.

The obvious approach is to add up the turn rate about the sensor's vertical axis. In the air the skater's spin axis is not the boot's vertical axis. The body tilts, and the skating leg is not aligned with the trunk. Call the angle between them theta. What the vertical axis measures is the true rotation times the cosine of theta.

At theta of 20 degrees the cosine is 0.940, so a triple that really turned 1080 degrees reads as 1015. That is 65 degrees of invented shortfall. At 25 degrees the cosine is 0.906 and the shortfall is 101 degrees, which is past the quarter turn line. The naive method does not merely add noise. It reliably tells a correctly rotated skater that they under-rotated.

The fix is cheap in code and free in hardware. Take the average turn rate vector across the flight, normalise it to get the actual spin axis for that jump, then add up the component of turn rate along that axis. It calibrates itself on every jump, which also means the pod shifting slightly on the boot does not corrupt the rotation figure.

3. The default sensor part saturates on a triple. Specify a wider one.

The part every tutorial reaches for reads up to 2000 degrees per second. A triple turns 1080 degrees in about 0.60 seconds, which averages 1800 degrees per second, and the rate is not flat: it peaks well above the average once the arms come in. A quad is 1440 degrees in about 0.70 seconds, averaging 2057 before any peak at all.

A saturated gyroscope does not report an error. It reports 2000 and keeps reporting 2000, and the integral silently comes out short. That is the same failure mode as change 2, from a different cause, and it would look like a real under-rotation. Specify a part rated to at least 4000 degrees per second, in the ST LSM6DSR or LSM6DSV16X class. It costs about ten dollars more.

4. Add a high range accelerometer, whose main job is to tell us it was not needed.

A landing puts the blade, then the boot, then the skater through a shock lasting a few milliseconds. Standard inertial parts stop at 16 g. We do not know whether a boot mounted sensor exceeds that, because the figures we can find are for sensors on the shank, above the boot stiffener, which sees far less.

So the first build carries a dedicated 200 g accelerometer alongside the main sensor. After the first ice session we will know the real peak. If it comes in under 12 g, the part comes off the second unit and saves 28 dollars. Spending 28 dollars to find out whether you need to spend 28 dollars is a reasonable trade when the alternative is a whole build with a clipped signal.

5. Two things missing from the parts list, both in the power chain.

The chosen controller board handles regulation and charging on its own, so the usual gap is not there. Two other gaps are. The card writes in bursts that pull 60 to 100 milliamps for a few milliseconds. At rink temperature a small lithium cell's internal resistance rises and those bursts sag the 3.3 volt rail, which resets the board mid session. A 100 microfarad bulk capacitor at the card holder, costing about a dollar, fixes it. It is absent from essentially every hobby parts list.

The second is charging speed. The board's onboard charger runs at 50 milliamps by default, so a 400 mAh cell takes around nine hours to fill. The board has a documented change to move it to 100 milliamps, halving that. Worth confirming against the schematic for the exact board revision before ordering, because an instrument that needs an overnight charge between two sessions in a day is an instrument that gets left at home.

What could go wrong

The wiring is a weekend. Telling a jump from a stumble is the project.

Soldering six wires and getting a card to write is a Saturday. The real work is the detector. A skater doing crossovers, a stroke into a three turn, a spin entry, a stumble, and a jump all look broadly similar to an accelerometer strapped to a boot. The detector has to find every jump the coach wrote down and almost nothing else, and that threshold cannot be a fixed number, because a 32 kilogram twelve year old and a 60 kilogram senior load the boot completely differently. It has to be set from each skater's own first few minutes on the ice.

Expect this to take three ice sessions of data and several rounds of tuning. Budget it as the largest single item of work on the project.

The safety valve, and the date it has to be pulled

The wide range gyroscope is the single point of failure. It is a less common part, and if it does not arrive, or arrives and does not behave, every triple in the data set is clipped.

The fallback is to narrow the claim rather than the project: validate only singles and doubles. A double turns 720 degrees in about 0.55 seconds, averaging 1309 degrees per second, which sits inside a 2000 degree per second part with room for the peak. All five acceptance numbers still apply, on a smaller set of jumps, and the limitation goes on the poster in plain words.

Decision gate: 24 October 2026. If the wide range part is not working on the bench by that date, the project switches to the doubles-only claim and does not look back. Parts ordered 25 August, every component bench tested alone by 12 September, turntable check passed by 26 September, first ice session 10 October, gate on 24 October, 60 jump validation set complete by 21 November.

And the dull one that actually ends projects

Condensation. The pod comes off the ice at around minus 3 degrees and goes into a lobby at 20 degrees and 50 percent humidity, where the dew point is about 9 degrees. Every cold surface inside the sealed shell is far below that, so water forms on the board, and it does it every single session. The answer is a gasket, a small desiccant sachet inside the shell, and a firm rule that the case is never opened at the rink.

Second dull one: the strap. If the pod rotates on the boot it does not ruin the rotation figure, because that calculation finds its own axis, but it does ruin the landing load axis and it eventually pulls the pod into the blade. Bond a plate to the heel counter with industrial adhesive and use the strap only as a backup.

For the specialist

Build detail

Working specification. None of it is needed to understand the project or decide whether to back it.

Flight detection and the state machine

Four states: ON_ICE, FLIGHT, IMPACT, SETTLE.

ON_ICE to FLIGHT when the acceleration magnitude falls below a per-skater threshold, set at 0.35 times that skater's median standing load, and stays there for 60 milliseconds. The 60 millisecond hold is what rejects the unloading phase of a crossover.

FLIGHT to IMPACT on an acceleration magnitude above 4 g with a rise time under 5 milliseconds. FLIGHT is discarded as noise if it lasts under 120 milliseconds, which is below any real jump, or over 1.2 seconds, which is above any human one.

IMPACT to SETTLE after 200 milliseconds, during which the peak is held. SETTLE back to ON_ICE once acceleration magnitude has stayed within 20 percent of standing load for 300 milliseconds. A fall is a flight whose SETTLE never arrives inside 2 seconds.

Per-skater thresholds come from the first 90 seconds of each session, taken as the median of the standing and stroking load. This replaces the fixed g threshold in the original brief, which cannot serve both a 32 kg and a 60 kg skater.

Rotation maths

Over the flight window, take the mean angular velocity vector, w_mean. Normalise it to get the unit spin axis n for that jump. Total rotation is the trapezoidal integral of the dot product of w and n across the window.

This is self calibrating per jump, so mount orientation does not enter the result. Residual error comes from nutation, where the axis itself moves during flight. That is second order and shows up in the validation number rather than being corrected for.

Error budget for a triple, 1080 degrees over 0.60 s. Gyro scale factor after the turntable calibration, 0.5 percent, gives 5.4 degrees. Bias stability at 0.05 degrees per second over 0.6 s gives 0.03 degrees, negligible. Axis wobble, estimated 1.0 percent, gives 10.8 degrees. Timing of the window edges at 5 milliseconds each end times 1800 degrees per second gives 9 degrees each. Summed in quadrature this is about 17 degrees, which is where the 20 degree acceptance figure comes from. It is a budget, not a measurement, and the video check is what decides.

Sampling, storage and bandwidth

Inertial sensor at 1600 Hz on all six channels. High range accelerometer at 3200 Hz on three. A landing shock peak lasts roughly 5 milliseconds, so a 200 Hz part would sample it once or miss it; 3200 Hz gives about 16 samples across the peak.

Data rate: (6 x 2 bytes x 1600) plus (3 x 2 bytes x 3200) equals 38.4 kB/s, or 138 MB per hour. A 90 minute session is 207 MB. A 32 GB card holds about 154 sessions, so the card is never the constraint and everything is logged raw. Writes go in 4 kB blocks roughly every 100 milliseconds.

No real time clock. Session time is relative to power on, and the phone stamps the absolute session start when the log is offloaded over the radio afterwards.

Power budget

Estimated draw: controller with the SPI bus active, 7 mA. Inertial sensor at 1600 Hz, 0.8 mA. High range accelerometer, 0.15 mA. Card idle plus write duty, about 1 mA average with 60 to 100 mA bursts. Radio off during a session. Call it 12 mA.

400 mAh divided by 12 mA is 33 hours at room temperature. At 0 degrees a small lithium cell delivers roughly 80 percent of rated capacity, giving about 26 hours, against a 90 minute session. The margin is large, which is intended, because the estimate is an estimate.

Acceptance: a measured 8 hour continuous run with the pod in a freezer at approximately 0 degrees, logging throughout, with no reset and no lost blocks. The calculation does not count. The freezer run counts.

Bring-up order

Every part alone before any two are joined. One: controller blinks and enumerates over USB. Two: card mounts, writes a 100 MB file, and the file reads back byte identical. Three: inertial sensor alone, held still, six faces, gravity reads 1 g on each in turn. Four: inertial sensor alone on the turntable, scale factor recorded. Five: high range accelerometer alone, dropped from 10 cm onto foam, peak recorded. Six: sensors and card together, checking the write bursts do not disturb the sensor readings. Seven: on battery, with a scope on the 3.3 volt rail during a card write, at room temperature and in the freezer. Eight: sealed in the shell, on a boot, off ice. Nine: on ice.

What this device cannot do

It cannot tell you which jump it was. An Axel, a Lutz and a flip are not reliably separable from one boot, and the device does not try. It reports revolutions and air time, and the skater or coach labels the jump.

It cannot judge a landing the way a judge does. Flow out of the landing, free leg position and posture are not visible to it.

It cannot measure spin travel or centering. See change 1.

It cannot read the edge. Roll angle at the boot mixes body lean with ankle flexion, so inside and outside edge cannot be separated with confidence.

It sees one foot. Anything about the other leg is inference.

It does not replace an official's call. It flags. It does not rule, and inside 30 degrees of the quarter turn line it says so.

It is splash resistant, not waterproof. It goes on the boot, not in a bag with wet blades.

Assumptions we made without asking

That the reader is a coach or a parent deciding whether to back a single builder over one term, not a company. That ice time is available roughly every two weeks. That the skaters in the validation set are at double and triple level, since singles do not stress any part of the design. That a phone capable of 240 fps can be borrowed, which is true of most phones sold in the last several years. That the device does not need to work in real time during a session, which is what allows all the analysis to be done after the fact, off the card.

The number to argue about first is the 20 degrees. Everything else on this page follows from it.
