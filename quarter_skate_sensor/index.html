Instrument · figure skating

Quarter

A pod on one boot. It measures how far a skater turned in the air, where on the ice it happened, and how cold that ice was.

It has to survive a landing, work in a rink at minus 3 degrees with no phone and no radio, and stay near a hundred dollars.

A skate blade cannot slide sideways. That is the whole physics of skating, and it is what makes the track possible: holding lateral speed at zero in the blade frame removes two of the three ways an inertial estimate drifts. The flight detector says when to switch it off.

The route claim is narrow. Twenty seconds holds; a four minute program does not.

Changes to the plan

Three things the two new features change

Satellites cannot trace a skating route. Cut them.

Three reasons, any one of them fatal. A rink is an indoor building with a steel roof, which puts the signal 20 to 30 dB under what a receiver needs, so usually there is no fix. Where there is one, consumer accuracy is 3 to 5 m against a rink 60 m by 30 m and a jump that covers about 8, so the error is the size of the thing being drawn. A 1 Hz fix also puts no samples inside a 0.6 second flight, and the receiver draws 30 mA against a 12 mA budget.

The ice reading is only as good as the window

An infrared thermopile reads the ice without touching it, and the geometry works: 150 mm up, 35 degree field of view, a 95 mm spot, and ice radiates almost like a perfect emitter. The failure is the window. A pod carried from a warm car into a rink condenses and then frosts, and a frosted window reads the frost. Add a stationary puck with a contact probe on the ice, and refuse any session where the two disagree by more than 0.5 °C. That still hands over a session number when the moving reading is thrown away.

This breaks the hundred dollar line, and the existing cut fixes it

The infrared sensor adds $15, taking a unit from $92 to $107. The cut was already written down: the 200 g accelerometer is there to prove 16 g would have been enough, and once six sessions have shown it the part comes off and the unit falls to $79. Build the first pair with it, everything after without.

What the two features make together

A map of the ice, not just a list of jumps

Ice temperature is not one number for a rink, and a skater carrying both sensors maps how it varies along the path skated.

What could go wrong

Heading is the hard part, not the wiring

Neither new feature is hard to build. Heading is. A rink floor is a grid of steel refrigeration pipe and the building above it is steel, so a magnetometer reads the building. That leaves yaw to the gyroscope alone, and yaw error turns straight into position error.

The safety valve is ultra wideband. If twelve passes have not held 1.5 m by the end of week eight, four anchors at about $30 each give 10 to 30 cm. They have to be mounted and powered around the rink, so ask in week one, not week eight.

And the dull one. The infrared window is 8 mm across and sits 150 mm above a surface that throws snow. One hard skid glazes it. Wipe it between runs, and log that it was wiped.

For the engineer

Build detail

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project.

Ice temperature

Sensor: MLX90632, I2C, medical grade band, 2 Hz in step mode.
Geometry: 150 mm nominal standoff, 35 degree field of view, 95 mm spot. Standoff varies with lean; anything over 250 mm is discarded because the spot starts to catch the boards.
Emissivity: ice 0.97, set in the sensor, no correction applied in software.
Soak: 15 minutes in rink air before the first reading is trusted. The die temperature sensor drives the compensation, and it lags while the pod is still cooling.
Reference: DS18B20 in the stationary puck, probe lying flat on the ice, 0.25 °C resolution mode.
Fixed point: stirred crushed ice and distilled water, 0.00 °C within about 0.02, before and after every session.
Gate: any pass inside 1 m of the puck must agree within 0.5 °C or the session's infrared data is marked unusable and only the puck number is kept.

Route and drift

Filter: error state Kalman filter on the existing 1,600 Hz inertial stream.
Constraint: lateral and vertical velocity held at zero in the blade frame during glide. This is the non-holonomic constraint used for wheeled vehicles, and it applies because a blade resists sideways travel.
Constraint off: during flight, from takeoff to landing, using the existing state machine. In the air the blade constrains nothing, and applying it there injects error.
Heading: gyroscope only. No magnetometer, for the reason in the risks section.
Loop closure: the skater starts and ends at the centre ice mark. Closure error is distributed backwards over the lap, which is what keeps the corrected track inside 1.5 m.
Boundary: track is constrained to the 60 by 30 m rink rectangle with 8.5 m corner radii.
Not attempted: absolute position over a full program, blade edge, or which foot. Only the traced shape of one pass.

Flight detection and the state machine

Idle, loading, flight, landing, settle. Takeoff is a drop in vertical load below 0.35 g held for 40 ms. Landing is a shock above 6 g with the load returning. Flight under 150 ms is discarded as a stumble, over 1.2 s as a data fault. Settle window of 300 ms before the next event can start.

Rotation maths

Turn rate integrated across the flight window about the axis of rotation found from the flight itself, not the boot's vertical axis. Trapezoidal integration at 1,600 Hz. Scale factor from the turntable run applied per axis. Reported as total degrees and as degrees short or long of the nearest whole revolution.

Sampling, storage and bandwidth

Inertial 1,600 Hz, six channels, two bytes: 19.2 kB/s, 104 MB in a 90 minute session. Infrared 2 Hz, four bytes: 29 kB per hour, which is nothing. Track is derived offline from the raw log, not computed on the pod. A 32 GB card holds a season. Raw is kept so any number can be re-derived after the fact.

Power budget

Controller with SPI active 7 mA, inertial sensor at 1,600 Hz 0.8 mA, high range accelerometer 0.15 mA, card about 1 mA average with 60 to 100 mA write bursts, infrared sensor 0.25 mA, radio off during a session. Call it 12.3 mA. 400 mAh gives 33 hours at room temperature and about 26 at 0 °C, against a 90 minute session.
Acceptance: a measured 8 hour continuous run with the pod in a freezer at about 0 °C, logging throughout, no reset and no lost blocks. The calculation does not count. The freezer run counts.

Bring-up order

Every part alone before any two are joined. Controller blinks and enumerates. Card writes 100 MB and reads back byte identical. Inertial sensor held still, six faces, 1 g on each. Inertial sensor on the turntable, scale factor recorded. High range accelerometer dropped from known heights. Infrared sensor pointed at the ice water bath, then at a surface of known temperature, then at ice. Puck probe in the same bath. Only then the whole chain, and only then on ice.

What this device cannot do

It cannot name the jump. It measures rotation, not which jump was attempted.
It cannot centre a spin. The physics does not allow it from one boot.
It cannot give absolute position over a whole program. Twenty seconds, corrected at a loop, is the claim.
It cannot measure air temperature or ice hardness. Surface temperature is a proxy for hardness, not a measurement of it.
It cannot read the ice through a frosted or snow glazed window, and it will not always know that it is frosted. That is what the reference puck is for.
It does not know which foot. One pod, one boot, and the other boot is invisible to it.

Assumptions made without asking

Rink is a standard 60 by 30 m sheet with the usual painted markings. If it is not, the video control points and the boundary constraint both need remeasuring.
Ice is held between about minus 6 and minus 2 °C, which is the figure skating range. Colder rinks are fine; the colour scale moves.
The skater will start and end runs at a marked spot. Without that the loop closure has nothing to close on.
The rink will allow a small puck to sit on the ice near the boards during a session.
