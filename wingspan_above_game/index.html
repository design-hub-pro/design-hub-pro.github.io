Wingspan Above

You fly one small migratory bird from the boreal forest to the tropics. Everything that kills real birds is in your way, and you feel it from inside the wings. A second mode, Roost, gives children with additional needs one bird that cannot die.

First-Person Flight Conservation Survival Sim Single Player Roost Mode

Most conservation games put you above the problem, managing a reserve from a map. This one puts you inside it. The camera sits behind a beak, between two beating wings, and the whole game is one migration: about 3,000 km, roughly 40 in-game days, one life. Fat reserve is your health bar. Wind is your terrain. The hazards are not invented monsters. They are glass towers, floodlights, cleared forest, and the distance between one wetland and the next.

The design bet is simple. A player who has spent six hours failing to cross a city at night will read a window-collision statistic differently afterward.

How it plays

Fat is the health bar

There is no damage meter. You burn fat to fly and rebuild it at stopovers. Head into a wind you cannot beat and you land short, in the wrong place, with nothing left. Almost every death in the game is a fuel decision made four hours earlier.

Wind is the level design

Each stage generates a wind field. Thermals lift you for free over sunlit ridges. Crosswinds push you off the coast. Waiting one more day at a stopover for a tailwind is a real choice against a shrinking food supply, which is exactly the choice real migrants make.

The hazards are the real ones

Reflective glass that shows you a sky that is not there. Floodlights that spin your compass and hold you circling until dawn. A stopover wetland that was drained between one season and the next. Outdoor cats in the last hundred metres. Nothing in the threat list is fictional.

One life, several seasons

Survive the southbound trip and you play the return. The world does not reset with you: the clearing you flew over in autumn is wider in spring. Progress is the journal filling up, not an upgrade tree. You do not get stronger. You get better at reading the sky.

SECOND MODE

Roost

A child adopts one bird, names it, and the bird comes back. It cannot die, nothing chases it, and there is no way to lose. Built for children with autism, anxiety, sensory processing differences, and limited or no speech.

Why this is a separate mode and not a difficulty setting

The main game is built out of the exact things that push these players out: permadeath, a resource meter that punishes you for a decision made an hour ago, a floodlight sequence that strobes, predators arriving without warning, and a story whose point is loss. Turning the difficulty down does not remove any of that. It just makes the same distressing structure slower.

So Roost keeps the world, the art, and the bird, and throws out the survival loop entirely. Same species, same migration route, same wetlands. Different rules about what can happen to you there.

How Roost works

The bird is yours and it has a name

The child names it on the first run and that name is used everywhere after. It has a fixed appearance, a fixed voice, and one personality. It is never swapped, upgraded, or replaced. For a lot of these players the whole value is in the fact that it is the same bird every single time.

It comes back at the same time every day

A grown-up sets the visit time. The bird arrives then, and the visit runs the same five steps in the same order: it lands, you fill the water, you put out food, you sit and watch, you say goodbye. The routine is the feature. Nothing is randomised, and the steps are shown as pictures so the child can see the whole visit before it starts and knows exactly when it ends.

You talk to it without speaking

A symbol board sits along the bottom: hungry, tired, scared, happy, play, rest. Tap one and the bird responds visibly — fluffs up, comes closer, tucks its head. The bird also uses the same board back at the child, which is the part that does the work. It gives a feeling a name, then shows what that feeling looks like on a body, in a situation with no social cost for getting it wrong.

The board should be configurable to match the symbol set the child already uses at school or in therapy. Shipping a private vocabulary would mean teaching a second language that works nowhere else.

Flying together is slow on purpose

The flight in Roost has no target and no route. You glide beside the bird over quiet water while a soft ring expands and contracts, and the wing beat is tied to that rhythm rather than to input. It is a wind-down activity for the end of a session. Roughly four minutes, ends on its own, no score.

When it migrates, it writes

Once a season the bird leaves for a few weeks of real time. This is the one hard thing in Roost, and it is handled carefully: the child is told before it happens, shown on a calendar, and the bird sends back a painted postcard from each stopover that the child can answer. Separation is survivable and temporary, and it is rehearsed here at a size the child can hold. A grown-up can turn the migration off completely.

Care in the game maps to care outside it

Planting a berry shrub in the Roost garden unlocks a card showing how to do the same thing on a real windowsill. Same for a water dish, a window decal, keeping a cat in at dawn. It is the one place where Roost and the main game land in exactly the same spot: a short list of small real actions.

Everything sensory is off by default

No sudden sounds, no flashes, no camera shake, no music sting, no failure buzzer. Motion is reduced, the palette is desaturated a step from the main game, and a grown-up can slow the whole pace further or skip the goodbye step. Defaults are set for the most sensitive player rather than the average one, so setup is opting into stimulation, not out of it.

What Roost is not

It is not therapy and it should not be sold as one. There is no claim here that it improves emotional regulation, communication, or anything else measurable. Paced breathing, visual schedules, and symbol boards are borrowed from practice because they are familiar to these children, not because this build has evidence behind it.

Before any of it goes near a child it needs review by an occupational therapist and a speech and language therapist, and testing with non-speaking children rather than about them. The migration absence in particular is a real risk. For some children a bird that leaves for three weeks will be distressing no matter how well it is signposted, which is why it ships switched off.

What the game is arguing

Bird declines are hard to care about because they are diffuse. No single window, no single light, no single cleared lot causes them. The loss is the sum. A migration is the only frame where a player experiences that sum in one continuous run, because a bird crosses all of it in six weeks.

So the ending is deliberately small. You land in the mangrove, the journal closes, and the last screen names the four things that nearly killed you and what turns each one off: patterned glass, lights out during migration season, one unmowed wetland edge, a cat kept indoors. Each of those is a decision a person watching the credits can actually make.
