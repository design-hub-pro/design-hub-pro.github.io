Wingspan Above

You fly one small migratory bird from the boreal forest to the tropics. Everything that kills real birds is in your way, and you feel it from inside the wings.

First-Person Flight Conservation Survival Sim Single Player

Most conservation games put you above the problem, managing a reserve from a map. This one puts you inside it. The camera sits behind a beak, between two beating wings, and the whole game is one migration: about 3,000 km, roughly 40 in-game days, one life. Fat reserve is your health bar. Wind is your terrain. The hazards are not invented monsters. They are glass towers, floodlights, cleared forest, and the distance between one wetland and the next.

The design bet is simple. A player who has spent six hours failing to cross a city at night will read a window-collision statistic differently afterward.

How it plays

Fat is the health bar

There is no damage meter. You burn fat to fly and rebuild it at stopovers. Head into a wind you cannot beat and you land short, in the wrong place, with nothing left. Almost every death in the game is a fuel decision made four hours earlier.

Wind is the level design

Each stage generates a wind field. Thermals lift you for free over sunlit ridges. Crosswinds push you off the coast. Waiting one more day at a stopover for a tailwind is a real choice against a shrinking food supply, which is exactly the choice real migrants make.

The hazards are the real ones

Reflective glass that shows you a sky that is not there. Floodlights that spin your compass and hold you circling until dawn. A stopover wetland that was drained between one season and the next. Outdoor cats in the last hundred metres. Nothing in the threat list is fictional.

One life, several seasons

Survive the southbound trip and you play the return. The world does not reset with you: the clearing you flew over in autumn is wider in spring. Progress is the journal filling up, not an upgrade tree. You do not get stronger. You get better at reading the sky.

What the game is arguing

Bird declines are hard to care about because they are diffuse. No single window, no single light, no single cleared lot causes them. The loss is the sum. A migration is the only frame where a player experiences that sum in one continuous run, because a bird crosses all of it in six weeks.

So the ending is deliberately small. You land in the mangrove, the journal closes, and the last screen names the four things that nearly killed you and what turns each one off: patterned glass, lights out during migration season, one unmowed wetland edge, a cat kept indoors. Each of those is a decision a person watching the credits can actually make.
