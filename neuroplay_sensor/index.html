NeuroPlay · Helmet impact sensor

A helmet sensor built so its numbers can be checked

A 42 gram module on the rear of the shell. It captures the full shape of every impact above 15 g and holds it until docked.

This page is the sensor only. The app, the dashboard and the recovery model are separate documents.

How it works

Six steps between the hit and the stored event

SenseADXL372, ±200 g, 3,200 Hz
Trigger15 g crosses the activity threshold
Buffer20 ms of pre-trigger history
CorroborateGyro confirms the head turned
Store100 ms window written to flash
SyncBluetooth upload on the dock

The buffer is the step that is not obvious. An accelerometer can only report an impact once it has begun, so the ADXL372 keeps a rolling 20 ms and returns what came before the trigger. Without it the peak is wrong.

How we know it works

The claim is 10% on peak acceleration, checked three ways

The project rests on one number: the peak resultant acceleration of an impact. Exposure counts, the recovery model and the dataset all inherit its error, so it gets checked first.

Scale, on a rotating arm

Clamp the sensor at a known radius on a motor arm, where acceleration is exactly ω²r. At a radius of 100 mm, 950 rpm gives 101 g. Shaft speed is read with an optical tachometer, independent of the sensor. Sweep 20 g to 180 g in eight steps, five runs each, 40 points. Acceptance: within 5% of ω²r across the range, and cross-axis leakage under 3%.

Dynamic response, on a pendulum

A helmeted head form takes a padded pendulum strike at three release angles and two impact sites, 30 impacts in total. A second identical sensor is bolted inside the head form at its centre of mass, and a shared trigger line syncs the two in hardware. Acceptance: peak within 10% and time-to-peak within 1 ms of that reference.

False events, by handling

Two hundred minutes of the helmet being put on, taken off, dropped, thrown into a bag and slapped. Acceptance: under one false event per session at the 15 g threshold. This is the test the project is most likely to fail, and the one worth reporting either way. If it fails, the threshold rises to 20 g and the gyro rule tightens before any field data.

Changes to the plan

Three things in the brief do not survive the arithmetic

The IMU clips on the first real hit

An MPU-6050 on an ESP32 is the usual pairing and it reads ±16 g. Football impacts run 20 to 100 g, and the ones this project cares about are the high ones. Above 16 g the output flat-tops and the peak becomes a censored value no software can recover. Use an ADXL372 at ±200 g. The gyroscope has the same problem in milder form: 40 rad/s of head rotation is 2,292 °/s, which saturates a ±2,000 °/s part on the severest impacts.

Continuous logging cannot work, so cut it

The brief asks for continuous measurement through practices and games. Six channels at 3,200 Hz and two bytes each is 38 kB/s, or 138 MB per hour, and streaming it holds the radio at roughly 180 mA, which empties a 400 mAh cell in two hours. Event-triggered capture stores 100 ms per impact instead: 3.8 kB an event, 115 kB for a 30-impact session. Standby falls to about 1.0 mA, so the unit runs two weeks between charges rather than half a practice.

It measures the helmet, not the head

A shell-mounted sensor is attached to the helmet, and the helmet moves on the head. Padding compresses and the shell rotates on the scalp, so the shell reads high by a factor that changes with where the hit lands. No single calibration constant fixes that. Relabel every output as helmet shell acceleration, publish the measured shell-to-head-form ratio as a finding in its own right, and stop short of claiming head kinematics. The instrument that couples to the skull is a mouthguard, and that is a different build.

What could go wrong

Telling a hit from a dropped helmet is the whole job

The hard part is not the wiring. Building the board is a weekend. Separating a real head impact from a helmet thrown into a kit bag is the work, and it has embarrassed helmet sensor systems before. The gyro rule and the hall sensor exist for that reason, and both need tuning against real handling data.

The safety valve is the ADXL372 itself, whose buffered impact mode is the fiddliest part of the build. If it is not returning clean pre-trigger data by the end of week six, switch to an H3LIS331DL at ±400 g and keep the schedule. That date is a decision, not a review.

The dull failure is the mount. Acrylic foam tape on a polycarbonate shell left in a car at 50 °C creeps, and a module that has shifted two millimetres has rotated its measurement axes. Check and photograph the mount every session.

For the engineer

Build detail

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project.

Trigger and capture parameters

Accelerometer: ADXL372, ±200 g, ODR 3,200 Hz, bandwidth 1,600 Hz, instant-on activity threshold 15 g on resultant.
Pre-trigger: 64 samples in the on-chip FIFO, 20 ms at 3,200 Hz.
Capture window: 100 ms total, 20 ms before the trigger and 80 ms after. 3 axes × 2 bytes × 320 samples = 1.9 kB accelerometer, plus 1.9 kB gyroscope, 3.8 kB per event.
Gyroscope: ICM-42688-P, ±2,000 °/s, 1 kHz, low-noise mode, running continuously into its 2 kB FIFO, which holds 333 ms of history. It cannot be woken by the accelerometer and still catch a 10 ms event, which is why it never sleeps.
Corroboration rule: an event is kept only if peak resultant rotational rate exceeds 300 °/s within 30 ms of the acceleration peak, and the hall sensor reads worn. Both thresholds are set from the handling dataset, not chosen in advance.
Filtering: SAE J211 CFC 180 applied offline, not on the device. Raw samples are stored.

Power budget

Cell: 400 mAh, 3.7 V nominal, 2C rated so it can supply the 350 mA radio peak.
Rail: AP2112K-3.3 at 600 mA with 100 µF bulk on the output. A 250 mA part such as the MCP1700 will brown out on radio transmit, which is the most common way this build fails on the bench.
The ADXL372 tolerates 3.5 V maximum, so it can never sit on the raw cell at 4.2 V.
Standby: ESP32-S3 deep sleep 8 µA, ADXL372 22 µA, ICM-42688-P 0.9 mA, flash standby 1 µA, regulator quiescent 55 µA. About 1.0 mA total, giving roughly 400 hours, or two weeks.
Per event: 40 ms awake at 80 mA, under 0.001 mAh. Three hundred events a season is negligible.
Sync: five minutes of Bluetooth at 30 mA is 2.5 mAh.
Charge weekly from the dock. Endurance is set by standby, not by impacts.

Calibration procedure

Low range: six-position static tumble against gravity sets zero-g offset and scale per axis to ±0.02 g.
High range: rotating arm at r = 100 mm. Target accelerations 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 130, 155, 180 g, requiring 420 to 1,270 rpm. Five runs per step. Fit a single gain and offset per axis, report residuals.
Cross-axis: spin each axis in turn, record the other two, require under 3%.
Timing: shared trigger line between the unit under test and the reference, checked against a square wave into both before every pendulum session.
The rotating arm gives sustained acceleration, so it calibrates scale but not dynamic response. That is what the pendulum is for.

Bring-up order

Test every component alone before wiring any two together.
1. Regulator on the bench with a 350 mA dummy load, scope the rail.
2. Charger alone, confirm termination and that the cell never exceeds 4.2 V.
3. ESP32-S3 blink, then deep sleep current measured, target under 15 µA.
4. ADXL372 over SPI, read the device ID, then static tumble.
5. ADXL372 FIFO and instant-on mode, verified by tapping the bench.
6. ICM-42688-P over SPI, device ID, then FIFO continuity for one minute.
7. Flash read and write, then a full erase cycle.
8. Hall sensor, then the whole event pipeline end to end.
9. Bluetooth sync last, because it is the only part that can be debugged with everything else already trusted.

Validation checklist

Static tumble, six positions, offset and scale within ±0.02 g.
Rotating arm, 40 points, 20 to 180 g, within 5% of ω²r.
Cross-axis leakage under 3%.
Noise floor at rest under 0.5 g RMS.
Timing sync against a square wave, under 1 ms skew.
Pendulum, 30 impacts, 3 energies × 2 sites, peak within 10% and time-to-peak within 1 ms of the internal reference.
Shell-to-head-form ratio recorded per site and reported, not corrected.
Handling dataset, 200 minutes, under one false event per session.
Repeat the tumble and one rotating-arm point after every 100 impacts to catch drift.
Battery endurance measured, not calculated, over one full week.

Changes four and beyond

Impact direction is reported as a shell-frame vector only, because the mount orientation on the head is not known to better than about 10°.
Cumulative season load should be reported as a count above stated thresholds, not as a single summed number, which has no accepted meaning.
Real-time alerting during play is out of scope for the hardware; the unit is not connected while worn.
A second unit on the shoulder pad would separate body impacts from head impacts, and is worth doing if time allows.

What this device cannot do

It cannot measure head acceleration. It measures the shell.
It cannot detect or diagnose a concussion, and no output should be presented as if it could.
It clips above 200 g linear and 2,000 °/s rotational. Severe impacts will be censored at the top end, and those events must be labelled as such in the dataset.
It cannot resolve impacts closer together than 100 ms; the second one lands inside the first capture window.
It does not measure impact location, only direction in the shell frame.
It is not certified for anything, has not been through NOCSAE testing, and adding mass to a helmet shell is a change no governing body has approved. Use it in practice settings with the coach's knowledge, not in sanctioned games.
The head form is plaster-filled and rigid, so it has no neck and no soft tissue. Absolute values from the rig are not equivalent to a NOCSAE headform. The comparison between the two sensors is valid; the absolute magnitudes are not.
