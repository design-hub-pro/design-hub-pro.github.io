Cadence

A pair of AR glasses and a smart band that measure how a person eats, not what they weigh. Bite rate, meal length, skipped meals, and stress at the table, recorded passively and shared only with a clinician the wearer chooses.

What it actually measures

Eating disorders are usually tracked through what a patient remembers and is willing to write down. That record is thin exactly when the illness is worst. Cadence measures the mechanics instead. The band's wrist IMU counts hand-to-mouth motions. A bone-conduction mic on the glasses arm counts chews. Together they give meal start, meal end, bite count, and bite interval. PPG and skin conductance add what the body was doing during those minutes.

From that you get four signals a clinician can use: meals eaten per day, how long each one took, how the eating rate changed inside a meal, and whether physiological arousal spiked around food. No calorie counting. No weight. Nothing that turns the device into another number to restrict against.

Two things worth cutting

Do not call it detection. A wearable cannot diagnose anorexia, bulimia, or binge-eating disorder, and a system that claims to will fail its first ethics review. Behaviour change over weeks is a screening signal that prompts a human conversation. The word in the product should be "flag", and the flag should go to a clinician, never to the wearer as a verdict.

Drop the always-on camera. A forward camera is the easiest way to identify food, and the fastest way to lose every participant. It records other people, in dining halls, without their consent. Recommendation: the camera fires only on a confirmed meal-start from the wrist IMU, captures a single frame, classifies it on-device, and discards the pixels within one second. If a review board still objects, ship without the camera. The chew and wrist signals carry the four core measures on their own.

There is also a real risk the device itself becomes a compulsion. Someone who counts calories will count bites. The mitigation is design, not software: the wearer sees no live numbers at all, only a weekly summary they open deliberately, and the coaching layer says "slow down" rather than "you are at 34 bites".

Build detail

Not needed to understand the project. For a mentor or engineer reviewing feasibility.

Sensors

Band: 6-axis IMU at 50 Hz for gesture recognition, green-light PPG for heart rate and RMSSD, two dry electrodes for electrodermal activity at 4 Hz, skin thermistor. Glasses: piezo bone-conduction contact mic on the temple arm at 8 kHz for chew detection, 6-axis IMU for head pitch, optional single-frame RGB capture. All fusion happens on the phone.

Models

Bite detection is a small temporal CNN over IMU windows. Chew counting is a spectral energy detector with a learned threshold per wearer, calibrated during a two-minute enrollment. Meal segmentation is an HMM over bite events. All three run quantised on the phone NPU, so the pipeline works offline and no raw audio or video leaves the device.

Power

Band: 180 mAh, targeting four days with PPG duty-cycled to once per minute outside meals. Glasses: 120 mAh split across both arms, targeting a full waking day because the mic only wakes on a wrist-triggered meal-start event. Both charge on one puck overnight.

Privacy and consent

Data lands in an encrypted store on the wearer's phone. Sharing with a clinician is a per-clinician, per-time-window grant the wearer can revoke, and revocation deletes the server copy. Audio and image buffers are never persisted. Any deployment needs IRB approval, a clinical partner for the flag thresholds, and a documented escalation path for a flag that indicates acute risk.

Validation plan

Stage one: bench accuracy against video-annotated ground truth for bite and chew counts, in a lab, roughly 20 participants without an eating disorder. Stage two: two-week free-living tolerability and wear-time study. Stage three, only with a clinical partner: comparison of device-derived meal regularity against the EDE-Q and clinician notes. Nothing on this page is a result. No data has been collected.

Assumptions

Wrist-IMU bite detection generalises across cutlery, chopsticks, and hands, which published work supports for cutlery and is weaker for hands. Wearers will keep the glasses on through meals, which is the largest untested risk in the whole design. Clinicians want passive behavioural data at all, which needs interviews before any hardware is built.
