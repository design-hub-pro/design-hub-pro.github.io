Bicycle safety instrument

Clearance

A unit that clamps to a bicycle seatpost and measures how much room drivers actually leave when they overtake. Every close pass gets a distance in centimetres, a location, and twenty seconds of video.

It has to work in rain, run for a whole day on a USB power bank, and produce a number that survives an argument. Nothing here needs a phone or a signal.

The point of it

A camera shows that a pass was close. This says how close.

Plenty of riders already carry rear cameras. Video proves something happened but it cannot give a distance, because a single lens has no depth. Councils, police, and insurers all want a number, and so does anyone trying to argue that one junction is worse than another.

So the camera is demoted to evidence and a rangefinder does the measuring. That one decision shapes every other choice on this page.

The threshold this is measured against is real. The UK Highway Code asks for at least 1.5 m when overtaking a cyclist at up to 30 mph, and more above that. Most US states with a passing law set 3 feet. The academic version of this device already exists: Ian Walker fitted an ultrasonic sensor to a bicycle in 2006 and recorded 2,355 overtakes to study what changes how much room drivers give.

Sources: Highway Code, rules 159 to 203 · Walker, Accident Analysis & Prevention 39(2), 2007

How it works

Radar says a car is coming. Ultrasound says how close it got.

The camera records continuously into a twenty second loop in memory and throws it away unless something happens. The rangefinder runs at 10 Hz all the time. The radar exists only to answer one question: is the thing off to my right moving, or is it a wall?

SenseA 24 GHz radar looks backwards and reports anything closing on the bike.
RangeA side-facing ultrasonic rangefinder reads the gap to the right, ten times a second, 1 cm resolution.
BufferThe rear camera holds a rolling twenty seconds of 1080p in RAM.
DecideA pass counts only if the range falls then rises inside 1.5 s and the radar saw motion.
CorrectSubtract the handlebar half width. Correct for air temperature. Discard if the bike was leaning.
RecordDistance, speed, GPS fix, and the twenty second clip go to the SD card.

That fourth step is what stops the device being useless. A parked car, a hedge, or a garden wall all sit about 80 cm from a rider and would otherwise log as a close pass every few seconds. A real overtake has a shape: the range drops, holds for the length of the vehicle, then climbs. A wall gives a flat line. The radar confirms it.

The section that matters

How we know it works

Any build can produce a number. The question a judge, a reviewer, or a council officer will ask is why that number should be believed. This is the part of the project that answers it, and it is worth more time than the wiring.

Test 1: the bench, against a tape measure

The unit is clamped to a bench. A flat plywood board 1.2 m by 1.5 m stands in for the flank of a car and is moved to tape-measured distances from 0.50 m to 3.00 m in 25 cm steps. Thirty readings at each step, at three air temperatures if the season allows.

Acceptance: mean error within 3 cm and standard deviation under 5 cm at every step out to 2.50 m. Beyond 2.50 m the requirement is only that the reading is monotonic, because a pass at 3 m is not a close pass and does not need precision.

Test 2: the car park, against chalk

The bike is held in a stand on an empty car park. A car is driven past at 20, 30, and 40 km/h at three marked lateral offsets: 0.75 m, 1.25 m, and 1.75 m from the bike's centreline. Ten repeats of each combination gives 90 passes. Ground truth comes from two independent places, a chalk line the near tyre tracks over and a tape measure read on the video afterwards, so a mistake in one is visible in the other.

Acceptance: 90% of the 90 passes within 10 cm of tape. And a second criterion that matters more than the centimetres: no pass is put on the wrong side of the 1.5 m line when the true clearance is outside the band 1.35 m to 1.65 m. The device exists to make a pass or fail call, so the call is what gets scored.

Test 3: the road, against a manual count

Three hours of ordinary riding on roads with a mix of speed limits, with the camera also writing continuous video for the duration. Afterwards a human counts every overtake on the video by hand and compares it to the event log.

Acceptance: at least 95% of real overtakes logged, and fewer than one false event per ten minutes. The false positive number is the hard one, and it will be the thing that fails first.

What happens if it misses

If test 2 comes in worse than 10 cm, the honest response is to stop reporting centimetres. The device then publishes three bins instead: under 1 m, 1 m to 1.5 m, over 1.5 m. That is still a useful instrument and it is still defensible. Reporting 1.43 m when the real spread is 25 cm wide is not.

If test 3 cannot get false positives under one per ten minutes, the fallback is in the risks section below, and it has a date attached.

Test 2 is the one to show a judge. Almost every hardware project at this level demonstrates that a device responds to something. Very few can put a tape measure next to it and state the error. That comparison photograph, with the chalk lines and the tape in frame, is worth more than any amount of enclosure finish.

Changes to the original plan

Four things in the brief we would change

Two are cuts, one is a number specified from the wrong reference point, and one is a part that has to be added or the accuracy target is unreachable.

Cut the rider warning

A rear alert sounds like the most valuable feature on the list. The arithmetic kills it. The cheap 24 GHz module in this budget detects a car at roughly 8 to 10 m. A bike at 20 km/h passed by a car at 50 km/h has a closing speed of 8.3 m/s, so the warning arrives about 1.2 seconds before the car is alongside. That is not enough time to do anything useful, and a rider who flinches at 1.2 seconds moves sideways into the vehicle that is already there. Garmin's Varia gets 140 m of range, and 17 seconds of warning, because it uses an automotive-grade radar that costs more than this entire build.

So the radar stays but its job is narrowed to confirming motion. No beeper, no handlebar light. If a warning is genuinely wanted, the right answer is to buy a Varia and run it alongside, not to build a worse one.

Cut the on-device vehicle classifier

Recognising car, van, or lorry from the video was on the list. A Pi Zero 2 W runs a small detection model at roughly 2 to 4 frames per second. At 8.3 m/s closing speed the vehicle covers about 2.5 m between frames, while the measurement that matters happens inside a 100 ms window at minimum range. Vision cannot see that window and the radar can. The classification adds several build weeks and improves no number the project reports. Classify later, off the bike, from the saved clips, if anyone still wants it.

The 1.5 m threshold is measured from the wrong place

The brief compares the sensor reading directly against 1.5 m. The sensor sits on the seatpost, close to the bike's centreline. The rider's widest point is the handlebar, and a road bar is around 44 cm across, so the outer end sits about 22 cm further right, with an elbow or a mirror sometimes further still.

Left uncorrected, every logged clearance is roughly 24 cm optimistic. A pass that came within 1.30 m of the rider's hand logs as 1.54 m and gets marked legal. The fix is one measured constant per bike, subtracted at logging time, recorded in the file header so it can be audited. Handlebar half width plus 2 cm.

The threshold itself should also stop being a single number. The Highway Code asks for 1.5 m up to 30 mph and more above it. The device knows the closing speed from the radar, so use 1.5 m below 50 km/h of vehicle speed and 2.0 m above.

Missing: temperature compensation, and it is not optional

The speed of sound in air is about 331.3 + 0.606·T metres per second. A rangefinder that assumes 20 °C and is used at 0 °C reports 1.56 m for a true 1.50 m. At 35 °C the same gap reads 1.46 m. That is a 9 cm swing from weather alone, against a 10 cm accuracy budget, before any other error source has been counted.

A DS18B20 probe costs about $4 and has to sit outside the enclosure, in the airstream, shaded from direct sun. Inside the box it will read the Pi, not the air. This is the single cheapest part on the list and the accuracy target is unreachable without it.

Two smaller additions in the same spirit: a real-time clock, because GPS takes 30 to 60 seconds to get a first fix from cold and events before that would carry no timestamp, and a supercapacitor power-fail board so a jolted USB connector cannot corrupt the file that is mid-write.

Build two, but not two of the same

A second full unit is $285 and mostly duplicates the expensive sensor. A better spare is a core: Pi, camera, SD card, enclosure, clamps, and wiring, about $101. The sensors unbolt and transplant in twenty minutes. Total $386. If the Pi dies the week before a deadline, or the enclosure cracks in a fall, the project loses an evening instead of a fortnight.

The cut line, if the budget is $200

Swap the MB1242 for a MaxBotix MB1000 at about $30 and the unit lands near $205. Be clear about what that buys: 2.5 cm resolution instead of 1 cm and much weaker rejection of road noise, so the 10 cm acceptance target in test 2 will probably fail and the device becomes a three-bin classifier. That is an acceptable project. It is a different one, and it should be described that way from the start rather than discovered in week eight.

Do not substitute an HC-SR04 at $3. Outdoors, on a moving target, its usable range collapses to about 2 m and its 15 degree beam catches the road surface. It is the right sensor for a desk and the wrong one for a road.

What could go wrong

The wiring is a weekend. The clutter is the project.

Soldering six modules to a Pi is two evenings and a multimeter. The real work is teaching the device that the 80 cm reading it just took was a parked car and not a driver. Urban cycling puts a solid object within two metres of a rider almost continuously, and a naive threshold on the rangefinder alone would log several hundred false close passes an hour.

The V-shaped range profile plus radar confirmation is the plan. It is a plan, not a certainty, and it should be tested in week three on a busy road rather than in week nine.

Safety valve

If false positives are still above one per ten minutes by the end of week six, buy a Garmin Varia RTL515 at about $200 and read its ANT+ radar broadcast with a USB stick, using it as the trigger instead of the LD2450. It sees 140 m and tracks closing speed properly, so gating becomes easy. The cost is the budget and some of the originality of the build. That decision has to be made in week six, not week ten. A build that eats its own timeline is the most common way projects like this fail.

The dull one

The seatpost clamp rotates. Nobody expects it to and it always does, usually when the bike is lifted onto a rack. A unit that has drifted 15 degrees nose-down puts the beam on the tarmac at 1.8 m and every reading after that is a lie that looks plausible. The fix is a witness mark drawn across the clamp and the post so a glance shows it has moved, plus a bubble level check in the appendix checklist, plus a startup self-test that rejects a session if the resting range to the road is not what it should be.

Second dull one: the transducer face must be open to the air. Ultrasound does not usefully pass through a plastic window. It mounts through the enclosure wall on its own gasket, which means a hole in the box that has to seal, and a face that collects road spray. Expect to add a small downward-facing shroud after the first wet ride.

Other people's number plates

This records vehicles and their plates in public. The log is the rider's own record of what happened to them, only twenty seconds either side of an event is kept, and nothing is uploaded anywhere. That is a defensible position in most places. Publishing clips, or handing the dataset to anyone else, is a different question and worth checking locally before it comes up rather than after.

For the engineer

Build detail

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project, and safe to skip.

Event detection: the exact rule

Rangefinder samples at 10 Hz into a 3 second ring buffer. A candidate event opens when the range drops below 3.0 m and the drop between consecutive samples exceeds 15 cm.

The candidate is confirmed as a pass only if all four hold: the radar reported a target with negative range rate within the preceding 2.0 s; the range trace falls, holds within a 20 cm band for at least 0.3 s, then rises above 3.0 m, all inside 1.5 s; the IMU lean angle stayed under 8 degrees throughout; and at least four consecutive samples were valid.

Reported clearance is the minimum range across the hold, minus the bike half width constant, after temperature correction. Corrected range = raw × (331.3 + 0.606·T) / c_assumed, where c_assumed is whatever the sensor firmware used.

Anything that opens a candidate and fails confirmation is counted in a rejects tally, not discarded silently. The rejects-per-hour figure is the fastest way to see the clutter problem getting worse.

What lands on the SD card

One CSV per session, one MP4 per event. Header carries the bike half width constant, the firmware version, the assumed speed of sound, and the boresight self-test result, so a file can be re-analysed later without guessing what it was recorded with.

Columns: iso_time, lat, lon, bike_speed_kmh, raw_min_m, temp_c, corrected_m, clearance_m, threshold_m, verdict, radar_closing_ms, lean_deg, clip_file, confidence.

Clip is 20 s at 1080p30, roughly 8 Mbit/s, about 20 MB. A 64 GB card holds around 3,000 events, which is about 50 hours of urban riding at a typical 60 overtakes per hour.

Power budget

Measured intent, to be confirmed on the bench in week two. Pi Zero 2 W with the camera encoding continuously, 400 to 550 mA at 5 V. Radar 70 mA. GPS 30 mA. Rangefinder, IMU, and clock together under 10 mA. Wi-Fi off in the field.

Average 600 mA at 5 V, so 3.0 W. Peak with Wi-Fi up for offload, 900 mA, 4.5 W, which any 2 A power bank supplies.

A 10,000 mAh bank at 3.7 V is 37 Wh, about 31.5 Wh after conversion losses at 85%. That gives 10.5 hours against a longest expected session of 3 hours. Margin 3.5 times. Do not drop to a 5,000 mAh bank to save weight; the margin is what covers a forgotten charge the night before a test day.

Mounting geometry and the boresight check

Sensor axis horizontal, perpendicular to the direction of travel, at 0.65 to 0.75 m above the road. With a 12 degree full beam at 0.70 m height, the cone first touches the road at 0.70 / tan(6°) = 6.7 m, well outside the 3 m working range.

Tilted 5 degrees nose-down that becomes 3.6 m, still tolerable. At 15 degrees down it is 1.8 m and every measurement is corrupted. Hence the startup self-test: with the bike upright and nothing beside it, the reported range must exceed 4 m. If it does not, the session refuses to start.

At 1.5 m the 12 degree cone spans about 31 cm vertically, which lands on the door panel of a typical car. That is the intended target surface.

Bring-up order

Every module alone on the bench, with its own test script and a written pass criterion, before any two are wired together. Skipping this is how a week disappears into a fault that turns out to be an I2C address clash.

Order: Pi boots headless and logs to SD. Camera captures and encodes for one hour without dropping frames or overheating. Rangefinder reads a tape-measured 1.00 m to within 3 cm, indoors. Temperature probe tracks a fridge and a warm room. Radar reports a hand moving towards it. GPS gets a cold fix outdoors. IMU reads level and 30 degrees. Clock survives a power cut. Then pairs, then the full stack, then the enclosure, then the bike.

Validation checklist

Bench sweep, 0.50 to 3.00 m in 25 cm steps, 30 readings each, board perpendicular. Repeat with the board at 15 and 30 degrees off perpendicular to see how a curved car flank behaves.

Temperature check, same fixed distance, at a cold morning and a warm afternoon, correction on and off, to show the correction is doing what the physics says.

Car park sweep, 3 speeds by 3 offsets by 10 repeats. Chalk plus tape as two independent ground truths. Video retained.

Road session, 3 hours, continuous video, manual overtake count afterwards. Report recall, false positives per hour, and rejects per hour separately.

Repeatability, the whole car park sweep repeated a week later after the unit has been removed and refitted, to catch mounting drift.

Rain, one session in real rain or under a hose, checking for water on the transducer face and condensation on the lens.

What this device cannot do

It cannot see the wing mirror. At 10 Hz and 8.3 m/s closing speed the samples are 83 cm apart along the road, and a protruding mirror occupies about 30 cm of that. The mirror is often the closest part of the vehicle and the part that actually strikes riders. The device measures the door, and the reported clearance is therefore slightly generous on most vehicles.

It cannot measure a pass at more than about 3 m usefully, and does not try.

It cannot distinguish a deliberate close pass from a careless one, or a wide lorry from a narrow lane. It reports geometry, not intent.

It cannot work in a bike lane with a solid kerbside barrier on the measurement side, because there is nothing to distinguish. Mounting it on the other side changes what it measures.

It is not evidence in the legal sense unless a court accepts the calibration, which is exactly why the test 2 results and the file header constants exist.

It does not warn the rider. See the cut above.

Assumptions

Where to correct us

Written without a conversation, so these were assumed. Any of them being wrong changes the page.

One builder, working evenings and weekends, over roughly a school term, with a deadline that does not move. A budget in the region of $300 to $400. A road bike with a 27.2 mm round seatpost and a 44 cm handlebar. Riding on roads with 30 to 60 km/h limits, in a temperate climate. No requirement to work at night, though the radar and rangefinder both do; the camera does not. No cloud service, no phone app, and no live upload, because none of them are needed to answer the question the device exists to answer.
