Bicycle safety instrument · second unit

The helmet unit

A 92 gram module bonded to the back of a helmet. It carries the camera and an impact sensor, and it reads number plates the seatpost unit cannot see. It does not measure the gap, and this page explains at length why it must not try.

The rule that shapes everything below: nothing goes on a head unless it is worth the mass, and nothing goes on a head if it needs to point in a fixed direction.

The idea

The helmet buys height. It does not buy a stable aim.

A module on the rear shell of a helmet sits about 1.47 m above the road. The seatpost unit sits about 0.70 m up. That extra three quarters of a metre is worth having, because it is the difference between photographing a bumper and photographing a number plate.

What the helmet cannot give is a fixed direction. A head yaws for a shoulder check, pitches to look at a pothole, and rolls in a corner. Any sensor that depends on knowing exactly where it is pointing gets ruined by that.

So the system splits in two along that line. Everything that needs a stable aim stays bolted to the frame. Everything that benefits from height and tolerates a bit of wobble goes on the helmet.

Bike keepsThe rangefinder, the radar, the GPS, the temperature probe. The measurement.
Helmet takesThe camera and a high-g accelerometer. The record of what happened.
LinkBluetooth. The bike unit sends an event ID; the helmet unit tags the same moment.

The two units keep independent rolling buffers, so the link only has to carry a short message and a timestamp. If Bluetooth drops, both still record, and the clips reconcile afterwards on the session clock.

The main finding

A rangefinder at helmet height points straight over the roof of a car

This is the thing to settle before any of the rest matters, because moving the whole sensor set onto the helmet is the obvious version of this idea and it does not work.

The height is the worst one available

A saloon car roof sits at roughly 1.42 to 1.50 m. The helmet module sits at about 1.47 m. Fired horizontally, the beam is inside the roofline band of most cars, so it sometimes catches a roof edge, sometimes a door pillar, and sometimes nothing at all. That is worse than being reliably wrong. It is non-deterministic, which means no correction can rescue it.

Aiming down fixes one problem and creates another

To land on a door panel at 0.90 m from a height of 1.47 m at 1.5 m lateral, the beam has to point 20.8 degrees down. At that angle it reaches the road surface at 3.87 m, which is still outside the 3 m working range, so on paper it is fine.

Now add a head. A rider looking 10 degrees down at the surface ahead puts the beam at 30.8 degrees, and the road comes up at 2.47 m. At 20 degrees down, which is an ordinary glance at a pothole, the road is at 1.70 m. The sensor then measures tarmac and reports it as a very close pass.

And yaw breaks it exactly when it matters

Rotate the head 20 degrees and the beam no longer crosses the car flank perpendicularly. It measures a slant range, 1.5 / cos(20°), which is 1.60 m for a true 1.50 m gap. At 30 degrees it reads 1.73 m. That is a 23 cm error against a 10 cm budget.

The error is not random, which is what makes it fatal. Riders shoulder-check precisely when a vehicle is overtaking. So the largest yaw errors land on the events that the whole instrument exists to record, and every one of them biases the reading generous. A device that systematically overstates clearance during close passes is worse than no device.

Correcting for it needs the head's angle relative to the bike, which needs an inertial sensor on both plus a shared heading reference. Magnetometers are unusable next to a steel frame and moving traffic, and two gyros integrated separately drift apart within minutes. There is no cheap fix here.

The section that matters

How we know it works

The seatpost unit is checked against a tape measure. The helmet unit makes two different claims, so it needs two different proofs, plus one that has nothing to do with electronics.

Test A: can a person read the plate, and out to what distance

The claim is that height makes a plate legible. That is checkable and the answer is a distance, not a yes.

A car is parked behind the bike at 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, and 20 m, in bright sun, overcast, and dusk. Five captures at each combination. The frames are then shown to two people who have not seen the plate, separately, in random order, and asked to write down what they read.

Acceptance: both readers correct on at least 90% of frames out to 12 m in daylight. Beyond that the requirement is only to report honestly where it stops working. A stated failure distance is a result. A vague claim of good plate capture is not.

The same test is run on the seatpost camera at 0.70 m, so the pair of numbers shows what the height actually bought. That comparison is the single most useful figure this page can produce.

Test B: does the impact log fire, and only when it should

Twenty staged events: the helmet dropped from 1.0 m onto a gym mat, knocked off a handlebar onto grass, and struck against a padded post, plus the bike laid down on its side with the helmet fitted to a foam headform.

Then three hours of ordinary riding that deliberately includes kerb drops, cobbles, a gravel path, and the helmet being thrown into a car boot at the end.

Acceptance: 20 of 20 staged events produce a saved incident file, and zero false incidents across the three hours. False incidents are the harder half. A helmet tossed onto a passenger seat generates a respectable acceleration spike.

Test C: the mount, with a spring scale

The module has to stay on over a rough hour and come off before it drags a head with it. Those two requirements bracket a number, and the number is checkable with a $12 spring scale pulling on the shell.

Design target: the pad holds above 30 N of shear so it does not shed on cobbles, and releases below 100 N so it is not a snag point. Ten samples, after a 24 hour cure, on a shell cleaned with isopropyl.

Stated plainly: this is a bench check with a spring scale, not a certification test, and it must never be described as one.

What happens if a test misses

If Test A only reaches 7 or 8 m, the honest response is to publish that distance and keep the unit, because 8 m is still far better than the seatpost camera manages. If Test B produces false incidents that cannot be tuned out, the incident log gets demoted to a flag on the video rather than a standalone claim. If Test C cannot hit both numbers with the same adhesive, the safety valve below applies and the camera comes off the head.

Test A is the one to show. Two photographs of the same number plate, taken at the same instant from 0.70 m and from 1.47 m, with the distance written underneath, settles the entire design argument in about four seconds. Build that comparison rig early, because if the difference turns out to be small, the helmet unit should not be built at all.

Changes to the original plan

Four more changes, on top of moving the rangefinder back to the bike

Continuous video will never read a plate. Add a triggered still.

The Camera Module 3 has a 66 degree horizontal field of view. At 15 m that spans 19.5 m of scene, so recording at 1080p gives 99 pixels per metre. A number plate character is about 79 mm tall, which comes to 7.8 pixels. Nobody reads that.

Taking 20 pixels of character height as the point where a person can read a plate confidently from a still, 1080p video is legible only to about 5.8 m. By then the vehicle is nearly alongside and the plate is out of frame.

The sensor itself is 4608 pixels wide. Capturing one full-resolution still, rather than relying on a video frame, gives 236 pixels per metre at 15 m and pushes the readable range to roughly 14 m. So the change is: video for context, and a three-frame full-resolution burst fired the moment the bike unit's radar first reports a closing target. The video no longer has to carry a job it cannot do.

An MPU-6050 cannot record a crash

The brief has the general purpose inertial sensor doing impact detection. Its accelerometer tops out at 16 g. A helmet certification drop test permits peaks up to 300 g, and reconstructed real cyclist head impacts commonly run into the tens and low hundreds. Every genuine impact would therefore log as exactly 16 g, and the one number the file contains would be the number the chip ran out at.

Add an ADXL375 at about $13, which reaches 200 g. Keep the MPU-6050 alongside for the gyroscope, since rotation matters as much as linear acceleration in head injury and the two chips together cost $17.

No cable from the bike to the helmet

Running the helmet unit off the bike's power bank saves 38 grams and doubles the endurance. It also puts a cable between a rider's head and a machine they may part company with. That is not a tradeoff worth having, at any weight saving. The helmet unit carries its own cell, and the link is Bluetooth.

Nothing straps through the vents

Vent straps are the cheap way to mount to a helmet and they put a hard point directly against the shell, loaded inwards. Use a curved adhesive pad bonded to the outer shell, which spreads the load and is designed to let go. It also survives being cleaned, which vent straps do not.

Then take the camera off the bike

If the helmet does the filming, the seatpost unit's camera is redundant. Removing it drops that unit from $285 to $260 and cuts its power draw and its storage churn. The cost is an operational rule that has to be written down: a session without the helmet unit records distances but no video, and cannot be used for the validation work.

The mass budget and the endurance budget are fighting

At 2.25 W the 2000 mAh cell gives 2.9 hours, which covers a normal ride with very little to spare. Moving to a 3000 mAh cell takes it to 4.3 hours, and takes the module to 109 g.

The recommendation is to build with the 2000 mAh cell and keep the larger one on the shelf. Ninety two grams is defensible on a 280 gram helmet. A hundred and nine is a conversation, and it should be had after the first long ride rather than before it, when the real endurance number is known instead of the calculated one.

What could go wrong

A modified helmet is not a certified helmet

This is the real risk on this page and it is not electrical. A helmet is tested and certified as it leaves the factory. Bonding 92 grams to the shell changes its mass, its rotational inertia, and its surface, and no certificate covers the result. The project should say so in plain words rather than hoping nobody asks.

What that means in practice is a set of self-imposed design rules, followed because they are sensible and not because a standard requires them: keep the added mass under about 110 g, mount low and to the rear where it is closest to the head's centre of rotation, keep the profile smooth with nothing protruding more than 25 mm, use an adhesive pad that releases rather than a strap that does not, and never drill the shell. A hole in expanded polystyrene is a crack waiting for a reason.

If this is a school or club project, this is also the paragraph a supervisor will read first, so it should be written before the build starts rather than after.

Safety valve

If Test C cannot hit both the 30 N and the 100 N numbers with the same adhesive, or if a supervisor is not satisfied on the mass question, the camera comes off the head and goes on a rack top or a saddlebag mount at about 0.95 m. That gives back roughly half the height advantage, so expect Test A to land near 8 or 9 m instead of 12. That call has to be made by the end of week five, because the printed shell, the cabling, and the whole mass argument all depend on it and none of them are worth doing twice.

The dull ones

Adhesive fails on a dirty shell. Helmets collect sweat, sunscreen, and whatever the last spray polish was, and none of that is visible. Clean with isopropyl, bond, then leave it 24 hours before riding. A pad that lets go on ride three costs a module and possibly a rider's confidence in the whole project.

A black module on a helmet in a car park in July gets hot. A Pi Zero 2 W throttles above about 80 °C and will slow down or drop frames. Print the shell in a light colour, vent it on the underside where rain does not fall, and check the temperature after a stationary hour in the sun rather than discovering it mid-test.

And the module points backwards at whatever the rider's head is doing. During a shoulder check to the right, the camera swings to look at the kerb. The clip of the closest pass of the day may be a very clear picture of a hedge. That is the price of the height and it cannot be designed away.

For the engineer

Build detail

Working specification. Not needed to understand the project, and safe to skip.

The link between the two units

BLE, with the bike unit as peripheral and the helmet unit as central, chosen so the bike unit can advertise while the helmet is off the head and charging.

At session start the bike unit pushes its session ID and a monotonic clock reference. Both units then run their own clocks and never rely on the link again for timing.

On a candidate event the bike unit sends four bytes: event ID and a flag for whether the radar target is confirmed. The helmet unit marks its buffer and fires the still burst. Round trip is around 50 to 100 ms, which is irrelevant because both keep a rolling buffer of 20 seconds.

If the link drops, both units carry on recording independently and the clips are matched afterwards on the session clock. A dropped link degrades the still burst timing, nothing else.

Still capture: resolution against distance

Camera Module 3, 66 degree horizontal field of view, 4608 by 2592 sensor. Scene width at distance d is 2·d·tan(33°), which is 1.299·d.

At 1080p: 1920 / (1.299·d) pixels per metre. At 15 m that is 99 px/m, so a 79 mm plate character is 7.8 px.

At full sensor width: 4608 / (1.299·d). At 15 m that is 236 px/m, so the same character is 18.7 px.

Taking 20 px of character height as the human-reading threshold, full resolution is legible to about 14.0 m and 1080p to about 5.8 m. The 20 px figure is a working assumption for this build and Test A exists to replace it with a measured number.

Burst is three frames at roughly 200 ms spacing, fired on first radar contact, which with the LD2450 means about 8 to 10 m of range. If the Varia fallback on the other page is taken, the burst can be timed properly from 140 m and this section gets much easier.

Power budget

Pi Zero 2 W encoding 1080p30 continuously with Wi-Fi off, about 450 mA at 5 V, so 2.25 W. Sensors and Bluetooth together under 20 mA.

Boost from a single cell at 3.7 V nominal, 88% efficient, draws about 0.69 A from the cell.

2000 mAh gives 2.9 h. 3000 mAh gives 4.3 h. Against a 2 h ride the smaller cell leaves 1.45 times margin, which is thinner than the 3.5 times the bike unit enjoys and is the reason the larger cell stays on the shelf as an option.

Over-discharge cutoff is not optional on a single cell. A LiPo taken below 3.0 V is scrap, and a Pi that browns out mid-write takes the file with it.

Incident detection rule

ADXL375 sampled at 800 Hz into a 5 second ring buffer. An incident opens when resultant linear acceleration exceeds 25 g for more than 3 ms, which clears kerb drops and cobbles in bench testing but is a starting value, not a finding.

Confirmation requires either gyroscope resultant above 1500 degrees per second within 200 ms, or the acceleration exceeding 25 g again inside 2 seconds, which catches a slide followed by a second contact.

On confirmation the unit writes the 5 second high-rate trace, the 20 second video buffer, and the last bike-unit event ID, then continues recording for a further 30 seconds.

Helmet-on detection uses the gyroscope idle floor. A helmet on a shelf is far quieter than one on a head, and sessions that start without the helmet being worn are flagged in the log rather than silently recorded.

Bring-up order

Same principle as the bike unit. Every part alone, with a written pass criterion, before any two are joined.

Order: Pi boots and logs. Camera encodes for one hour on the bench without dropping frames. Camera captures a full-resolution still while encoding, which is the one function that could turn out not to be possible and should therefore be proven in week one. ADXL375 reads 1 g at rest on each axis and survives a tap. Gyroscope reads zero at rest. Cell, charger, and cutoff cycled twice. Bluetooth link to the bike unit. Then the shell, then the pad, then the helmet, then the road.

What this unit cannot do

It is not a concussion instrument and no number it produces should be described as a head injury measurement. It records that something happened and roughly how hard, on a sensor bonded to a shell, with no headform calibration behind it.

It cannot measure clearance. Without the bike unit it is a camera with an accelerometer.

It cannot read a plate at night or in heavy rain. Neither can any camera at this price, and the honest thing is to report the daylight number and stop.

It cannot record what the rider was not looking at. A shoulder check points it at the kerb.

It cannot make the helmet safer. It adds mass to a safety device in exchange for evidence, and that trade should be stated every time the project is presented rather than left for someone else to raise.

Assumptions

Where to correct us

Carried over from the seatpost brief, plus three specific to this unit. An adult road helmet of about 280 g with a smooth rear shell area large enough for a 70 by 45 mm pad. A rider whose helmet-mounted module sits near 1.47 m above the road, which a 1.75 m rider on a road bike roughly gives; a shorter rider or an upright town bike shifts every height number on this page and the beam argument should be re-checked, though its conclusion does not change. And a 20 pixel character height as the point at which a person reads a plate, which is a working figure taken from common practice rather than a standard, and which Test A is designed to replace.

This unit is half a system. The measurement lives on the other page.
